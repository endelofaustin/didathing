What to Look for and Expect in the Gospels. A Brief Instruction, by Martin Luther, 1522.

It has become a firmly established custom to count the Gospels according to the books and to say that there are four Gospels. As a result, people no longer know what Saint Paul and Saint Peter are saying in their letters, and their teaching is regarded almost as an addition to the teaching of the Gospels, as one may also gather from a preface by Jerome.

Worse still is another habit: people treat the Gospels and the apostolic letters as though they were law books, from which we are supposed to learn what we must do. Christ's works are held up to us merely as examples. When these two mistaken notions remain in the heart, neither Gospel nor letter can be read usefully or in a Christian way; people remain nothing but pagans, just as before.

Therefore we must understand that there is only one Gospel, although it has been written down by many apostles. Every letter of Paul and Peter, and the Acts of the Apostles written by Luke, is a Gospel, even though they do not all recount every work and word of Christ, and one says less than another. After all, not even one of the four great Gospels contains all the works and words of Christ, nor does it need to.

The Gospel is, and ought to be, nothing other than an account or history of Christ. It is like the way people write a book about a king or prince, telling what he did, said, and suffered in his lifetime. Such a story can be told in different ways, one writer at length and another more briefly.

So the Gospel is nothing other than a chronicle, history, or narrative of Christ: who he is, what he did, what he said, and what he suffered. One writer describes this briefly, another at greater length; one in this way, another in that way.

To put it as briefly as possible, the Gospel is the message about Christ: that he is the Son of God, that for our sake he became man, died, and rose again, and that he has been made Lord over all things.

This is what Saint Paul takes up and unfolds in his letters. He leaves aside many of the miracles and events of Christ's life described in the four Gospels, and yet he fully and richly includes the whole Gospel. We can see this clearly and beautifully in his greeting to the Romans, where he tells us what the Gospel is: 'Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle and appointed to the Gospel of God, which he promised beforehand through his prophets in the Holy Scriptures, concerning his Son, who was born of the seed of David according to the flesh, and declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by the resurrection from the dead: Jesus Christ our Lord,' and so forth (Romans 1:1-4).

You can see, then, that the Gospel is the history of Christ, the Son of God and of David, who died, rose again, and was appointed Lord. This, in sum, is the whole Gospel. Just as there is only one Christ, so there is and can be only one Gospel. Since Paul and Peter likewise teach nothing other than Christ in the way just described, their letters can be nothing other than the Gospel.

Indeed, the prophets also proclaimed the Gospel and spoke of Christ, as Saint Paul says here and as everyone knows. Wherever the prophets speak of Christ, their teaching is nothing other than the true, pure, genuine Gospel, just as if Luke or Matthew had written it. For example, when Isaiah says that Christ would die for us and bear our sins (Isaiah 53:4 and following), he has written the pure Gospel.

And I say this truly: unless a person understands the Gospel in this way, that person will never be enlightened in the Scriptures or grasp their true foundation.

Second, take care not to turn Christ into another Moses, as though he did nothing beyond teaching and giving examples, as the other saints do, or as though the Gospel were a book of instruction or a book of law. Therefore you must understand Christ, his words, his works, and his suffering in two ways.

First, as an example set before you, which you ought to follow and imitate, as Saint Peter says: 'Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example.' Thus, when you see that he prays, fasts, helps people, and shows love, you ought to do the same for yourself and for your neighbor. But this is the lesser part of the Gospel, and it is not yet what properly deserves the name Gospel. For in this way Christ benefits you no more than another saint.

His life remains his own and has not yet helped you at all. In short, this way of preaching does not make a Christian; it merely makes a hypocrite. Something far greater must happen to you. Nevertheless, for a long time this has been considered the best way to preach, even though it has been practiced only rarely.

The chief point and foundation of the Gospel is this: before you take Christ as an example, you must first receive and recognize him as a gift and present given to you by God, so that he is your very own. Then, when you see or hear that he does or suffers anything, you do not doubt that Christ himself, together with that doing and suffering, belongs to you. You may rely upon it no less than if you had done it yourself, indeed, as though you yourself were that very Christ.

See, this is what it means truly to know the Gospel. Here is the overflowing goodness of God, which no prophet, apostle, or angel has ever been able to express fully, and which no heart has ever been able sufficiently to marvel at or comprehend. It is the great fire of God's love toward us. By it the heart and conscience become joyful and at peace. This is what it means to preach the Christian faith.

That is why such preaching is called Gospel, which in German means a joyful, good, comforting message. And it is from this message that the apostles are called messengers. Isaiah says, 'To us a child is born, to us a son is given' (Isaiah 9:6). If he has been given to us, he must belong to us; therefore we must also receive him as our own.

Romans 8:32 likewise says, 'How shall he not also give us all things with his Son?' See, if you receive Christ in this way, as a gift made your own, and do not doubt it, you are a Christian. Such faith delivers you from sin, death, and hell and enables you to overcome all things. Oh, no one can speak enough about this! It is a tragedy that this preaching has been silenced in the world, even while people boast every day about the Gospel.

Once, then, you have Christ as the foundation and greatest treasure of your salvation, the other part follows: you also take him as an example and give yourself to serving your neighbor, just as you see that Christ has given himself to you. See how faith and love then go into action together! God's commandment is fulfilled, and the person is joyful and unafraid in doing and suffering all things. Therefore pay close attention: Christ as gift nourishes your faith and makes you a Christian.

Christ as example, however, nourishes your works. Those works do not make you a Christian; rather, they flow from you after you have already been made a Christian. As different as gift and example are, so different are faith and works. Faith possesses nothing of its own, but only Christ's work and life. Works have something of yours in them; yet even these are not for you but belong to your neighbor.

You see, then, that the Gospel is not properly a book of laws and commandments demanding our deeds. It is a book of divine promises, in which God promises, offers, and gives us all his blessings and benefits in Christ. That Christ and the apostles also give much good instruction and explain the Law is itself to be counted among his blessings, like the rest of Christ's works, for sound teaching is no small benefit.

This is why we see that Christ does not drive and compel people with terrors, as Moses does in his books and as commandments commonly do. Instead, Christ teaches gently and kindly. He simply explains what should be done or avoided and what will happen to evildoers and to those who do good. He forces and coerces no one. Indeed, his teaching is so gentle that it invites rather than commands, beginning, 'Blessed are the poor; blessed are the meek,' and so on.

The apostles also commonly use words such as 'I urge,' 'I ask,' and 'I appeal.' Moses, on the other hand, says, 'I command' and 'I forbid,' and adds terrible threats of punishment and suffering. With this instruction you will be able to read and hear the Gospels fruitfully.

When you open the Gospel book and read or hear that Christ comes to this place or that, or that someone is brought to him, understand that this portrays the preaching of the Gospel by which Christ comes to you or you are brought to him. To preach the Gospel is nothing other than to have Christ come to us, or to bring us to him.

And when you see how he works and helps everyone he meets, and everyone brought to him, know that faith works the same way in you: through the Gospel he offers your soul the very same help and goodness. If you remain still and allow him to do you good—that is, if you believe that he is doing good to you and helping you—then you certainly possess it. Christ is yours and has been given to you as a gift.

Afterward you must make an example of this and likewise help your neighbor, acting so that you too may be given to your neighbor as gift and example. Isaiah says: 'Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. Speak to the heart of Jerusalem and call to her that her sin is forgiven, her guilt has come to an end, and she has received double from the Lord's hand for all her sins' (Isaiah 40:1 and following).

These two blessings are the two aspects of Christ: gift and example. They are also signified by the two portions of the inheritance which the Law of Moses assigns to the firstborn son, as well as by many other figures.

Yet it is a sin and a shame that we Christians have come to this, and have been so careless about the Gospel, that we not only fail to understand it but must first have other books and explanations to tell us what to seek and expect within it.

For the Gospels and the letters of the apostles were written to be guides themselves, directing us into the writings of the prophets and Moses in the Old Testament. There we should read and see how Christ was wrapped in swaddling clothes and laid in a manger—that is, how he is contained within the writings of the prophets.

This is where our study and reading ought to be exercised: to see who Christ is, why he was given, how he was promised, and how all Scripture points to him. Christ himself says in John 5:46, 'If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote of me.' Likewise: 'Search the Scriptures, for they testify of me.' This is what Saint Paul means in Romans 1:2, where he says at the beginning of his greeting that God promised the Gospel beforehand through the prophets in the Holy Scriptures.

Thus the evangelists and apostles continually direct us into the Scriptures and say, 'So it is written,' or, 'This happened so that the Scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled.' And in Acts 17:11, Luke says that those who eagerly received the Gospel searched and examined the Scriptures day by day to see whether these things were true.

Likewise, when the apostle writes, he says near the beginning: 'Concerning this salvation the prophets searched and inquired, who prophesied of the grace that was to come to you, seeking to know what person or time the Spirit of Christ within them was indicating when he foretold the sufferings of Christ and the glories that would follow. It was revealed to them that they were serving not themselves but us in these things, which have now been announced to you by those who preached the Gospel to you through the Holy Spirit sent from heaven—things into which angels long to look' (1 Peter 1:10–12).

What does Saint Peter intend by this, except to lead us into the Scriptures? It is as though he were saying: 'We preach the Scriptures and open them to you through the Holy Spirit, so that you yourselves may read and see what they contain and of what time the prophets wrote.' Peter also says in Acts 3:24 that all the prophets who spoke, from Samuel onward, foretold these days. Thus Luke says in Luke 24:45 that Christ opened the apostles' understanding so that they could understand the Scriptures.

And Christ says in John 10 that he is the door: through him a person must enter. Whoever enters through him has the door opened by the doorkeeper, the Holy Spirit, and finds pasture and salvation. Thus it is finally true that the Gospel itself points us toward the Scriptures and instructs us in them, just as I have sought in this preface to point to the Gospel and give instruction about it.

But see what fine, delicate, pious children we are! So that we will not have to study the Scriptures and learn Christ there, we count the entire Old Testament as nothing, as though it were finished and no longer had any force. Yet it is precisely the Old Testament that bears the name Holy Scripture. The Gospel, properly speaking, is not supposed to remain merely a written document but is the spoken Word that brings the Scriptures to life, just as Christ and the apostles did.

For that reason Christ himself wrote nothing, but spoke. He called his teaching not 'Scripture' but 'Gospel'—that is, good news or proclamation—which should be carried forward not only by the pen but by the mouth. Yet we rush in and turn the Gospel into a law book, a lesson in commandments; we turn Christ into another Moses, and the Helper into nothing but a teacher. What judgment might God not allow to fall upon such a foolish and perverse people?

It is only fitting that God should have allowed us to fall into the teachings of the pope and the lies of men, since we abandoned his Scriptures and had to study, instead of Holy Scripture, the decrees of a deceitful fool and wicked scoundrel. Oh, if only God would grant that my work should soon become neither useful nor necessary! Then there would surely be hope that Holy Scripture had been restored to its proper dignity. Enough has now been said, as briefly as possible, for this preface and instruction. We shall say more in the exposition. Amen.
