The Benefit of Christ’s Death. A modern English translation of the sixteenth-century Italian work by Benedetto da Mantova.


To Christian Readers.


A work has come into our hands that is among the most devout and learned writings produced in our time. Its title is The Benefit of Jesus Christ Crucified for Christians. We have thought it good to print it for your comfort and benefit, without naming its author, so that the message itself, rather than the authority of the person who wrote it, may move you.


Chapter 1 — Original Sin and the Misery of Humanity.


Holy Scripture tells us that God created humanity in his own image and likeness. In the body, he made man free from suffering; and in the soul, righteous, truthful, devout, merciful, and holy.

But man was overcome by the desire for knowledge and ate the fruit that God had forbidden. He lost that divine image and likeness and became like the beasts and like the devil who had deceived him. In his soul, he became unjust, deceitful, cruel, irreverent, and hostile to God. In his body, he became subject to suffering and to a thousand troubles and illnesses, becoming not merely like the unreasoning animals, but in some respects lower than they.

If our first parents had obeyed God, they would have passed on their righteousness and holiness to us as an inheritance. But because they disobeyed him, they have left us an inheritance of unrighteousness, irreverence, and hostility toward God. Consequently, it is impossible for us, by our own strength, to love God and conform ourselves to his will. Instead, we are his enemies. Because he is a just judge who punishes our sins, we cannot of ourselves trust in his mercy.

In short, through Adam's sin our entire nature was corrupted. Once it was placed above all other creatures; now it is subject to them all, enslaved to the devil, to sin, and to death, and condemned to the misery of hell.

Our judgment was completely lost. We began to call good evil and evil good, accepting falsehoods as truth and rejecting truths as false. Reflecting on this, the prophet declares that every human being is a liar and that there is no one who does good. Meanwhile, the devil rules in peace, like a strong man armed who guards his palace: that is, this world, over which he has become prince and ruler.

No tongue could express even a thousandth part of our wretchedness. Although God created us with his own hands, we have lost his divine image and have become like the devil, sharing his nature and becoming one with him, desiring whatever he desires and rejecting whatever displeases him. Because we have fallen prey to so wicked a spirit, there is no sin so grave that any one of us would not be ready to commit it, if God's grace did not restrain us.

This loss of righteousness, together with the inclination and readiness to commit every kind of injustice and irreverence, is called original sin. We carry it with us from our mother's womb, being born children of wrath. It began with our first parents and is the cause and fountain of all the vices and wicked deeds we commit. If we want to be delivered from these things, to return to our former innocence, and to recover God's image, we must first recognize our own misery.

No one seeks out a physician without first knowing that he is ill. Nor does anyone understand the excellence of a physician, or the gratitude owed to him, without realizing that his illness is deadly. In the same way, no one truly knows Christ, the only physician of our souls, without recognizing that his soul is sick. Nor can anyone appreciate Christ's excellence or the gratitude owed to him without coming to understand the seriousness of his own sins and the deadly sickness we have inherited through the corruption of our first parents.


Chapter 2 — Why God Gave the Law: That We Might Know Our Sin and Seek the Righteousness of Faith.


In his infinite goodness and mercy, our God determined to send his only Son to set the miserable children of Adam free. Knowing that they must first become aware of their misery, he chose Abraham and promised that all nations would be blessed through his offspring. He took Abraham's descendants as his own particular people. After bringing them out of Egypt and freeing them from Pharaoh's slavery, God gave them the Law through Moses.

That Law forbids sinful desire and commands us to love God with all our heart, all our soul, and all our strength. It requires us to place our whole hope in God and to be ready, for his sake, to surrender our lives, to endure every kind of bodily torment, and to give up all possessions, status, and honors in order to honor him. We must choose death rather than do even the smallest thing that displeases our God, and do all this joyfully and willingly.

The Law also commands us to love our neighbors as ourselves. By neighbor it means every kind of person, friends and enemies alike. It requires us to be ready to do for everyone what we would wish them to do for us, and to care for everything belonging to others as we care for our own. When a person looks into this holy Law as into a clear mirror, he immediately recognizes his weakness and inability to obey God's commandments or to give his Creator the love and honor that are due to him.

The Law's first task, then, is to reveal sin, just as Paul says. Elsewhere he writes, "I would not have known sin except through the Law."

The Law's second task is to make sin increase. We have turned away from obedience to God, become servants of the devil, and become filled with corrupt passions and desires. Therefore we cannot bear to have God forbid the very desires that grow stronger when they are prohibited.

Thus Paul says that sin became sinful beyond measure. He also says, "Sin was dead; but when the commandment came, sin came to life and increased."

The Law's third task is to reveal God's wrath and judgment. God threatens death and eternal punishment to those who do not keep his Law perfectly. For Scripture says, "Cursed is everyone who does not continue in everything written in the book of the Law." This is why Paul calls the Law a ministry of death and says that it brings wrath.

Having exposed sin, increased it, and revealed God's wrath and judgment, with their threat of death, the Law performs its fourth task: it terrifies a person. He falls into despair and longs to satisfy the Law, but plainly sees that he cannot. Because he cannot fulfill it, he grows angry with God and wishes God did not exist, fearing that God will condemn and severely punish him. As Paul says, the mind set on the flesh is hostile to God, because it does not submit to God's Law and indeed cannot do so.

The Law's fifth task is its proper purpose, its most excellent and necessary work: to drive a person to Christ. In the same way, when the Israelites were terrified, they begged Moses, "Do not let the Lord speak to us, lest we die. Speak to us yourself, and we will listen and do everything." And the Lord answered, "They have spoken well."

They were commended for one reason: they had asked for a mediator between themselves and God. Moses was that mediator, pointing ahead to Jesus Christ, who was to become our advocate and mediator with God. Therefore God said to Moses, "I will raise up for them a prophet like you from among their brothers. I will put my words in his mouth, and he will tell them everything I command him. I will hold accountable anyone who does not listen to the words he speaks in my name."



Chapter 3 — The Forgiveness of Sins, Our Justification, and Our Entire Salvation Depend on Christ.


Our God has now sent the great prophet he promised us: his only Son. He sent him to free us from the curse of the Law, reconcile us to God, restore our will so that we may do good works, heal our freedom of choice, and renew in us the divine image that we lost through our first parents' sin. Since we know that there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we can be saved than the name of Jesus Christ, let us run to him with the steps of living faith and throw ourselves into his arms. For he calls out to us, "Come to me, all you who labor and are weighed down, and I will give you rest."

What comfort or joy in this life could compare with that of someone crushed under the unbearable weight of his sins, who hears such sweet and gentle words from the Son of God, offering so kindly to refresh him and free him from his terrible burden? Yet everything depends on our truly recognizing our weakness and misery, for no one appreciates what is good until he has felt what is evil.

For that reason Christ says, "If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me and drink." It is as though he were saying that a person who does not know himself to be a sinner, who does not thirst for righteousness, cannot taste how sweet our Jesus Christ is, or how delightful it is to think of him, speak of him, and follow his most holy life.

If, then, the Law has made us aware of our weakness, John the Baptist points us toward the most compassionate physician, saying, "Behold the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world." He is the one who frees us from the heavy yoke of the Law, abolishing its curses and stern threats, healing all our infirmities, renewing our will, restoring us to our former innocence, and rebuilding the image of God within us. For, as Paul says, just as all die in Adam, so all are made alive in Christ. We must not imagine that Adam's sin, which we inherited, has greater power than Christ's righteousness, which we likewise receive by faith.

A person might once have complained that, through no act of his own, he was born and conceived in sin because of the unrighteousness of his parents, through whom death came to reign over everyone. But now all cause for complaint has been taken away. For in the very same way, through no doing of our own, Christ's righteousness has come to us, and eternal life has come through Christ, who has destroyed death.

Paul gives a wonderful explanation of this, which I will set down here:

"Just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death spread to all people, because all sinned. Sin was in the world before the Law was given, though sin is not counted in the same way where there is no Law. Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over those who had not sinned by committing a transgression like Adam's. Adam was a pattern of the one who was to come.

"But the gift is not like the trespass. For if many died through the sin of one man, how much more has God's grace, and the gift that comes by the grace of one man, Jesus Christ, overflowed to many! Nor is the gift like the result of that one man's sin. Judgment followed one trespass and brought condemnation, but the gift followed many trespasses and brought justification. For if death reigned through the one man because of his trespass, how much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one man, Jesus Christ!

"So, just as one trespass brought condemnation to all, so one righteous act brings justification and life. Just as the disobedience of one man made many sinners, so the obedience of one man will make many righteous. The Law came in so that sin might increase; but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more. Thus, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness and bring eternal life through Jesus Christ."

From these words of Paul we clearly learn what we said earlier: that the Law was given so that sin might be known. We also learn that Adam's sin is not stronger than the righteousness of Christ by which we are justified before God. Just as Christ is greater than Adam, so Christ's righteousness is stronger than Adam's sin. If Adam's sin was enough to make us sinners and children of wrath before we committed any sinful act of our own, how much more is Christ's righteousness enough to make us righteous and children of grace without any good works of our own? Our works cannot be good unless we ourselves have first been made good and righteous through faith, as Augustine also teaches.

This reveals the error of those who, because they have committed some grave sins, despair of God's kindness and think he will not pardon, cover, and forgive even the greatest offenses. For God has already punished all our sins and all our wrongdoings in his only Son, and in this way has proclaimed a general pardon to the whole human race. Everyone who believes the gospel receives its benefit—the wonderfully good news that the apostles announced throughout the world, saying, "We appeal to you on Christ's behalf: be reconciled to God. For God made the one who knew no sin to be sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."

Isaiah foresaw this immeasurable kindness of God and wrote these divine words. They portray the suffering of our Lord Jesus Christ, and its purpose, so clearly that no better description is found even in the writings of the apostles:

"Who has believed what we have heard? To whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed? He grew up before him like a tender shoot, like a root out of dry ground. He had no beauty or majesty to attract us to him. We saw him, yet there was nothing in his appearance that made us desire him. He was despised and rejected by people, a man of sorrows, familiar with suffering. He carried our infirmities and bore our sorrows. Yet we thought he was wounded, struck down, and afflicted by God. But he was pierced for our wrongdoings and crushed for our sins. He was punished so that we might have peace, and by his wounds we are healed. All of us have wandered away like sheep; each of us has turned to his own way, and the Lord has laid on him the wrongdoing of us all. He was oppressed and mistreated, yet he did not open his mouth. He was led like a lamb to slaughter, and like a sheep silent before its shearers, he did not open his mouth."

What terrible ingratitude! How outrageous it would be if, while professing to be Christians and knowing that the Son of God took all our sins upon himself and blotted them out by his precious blood—allowing himself to be punished for us on the cross—we nevertheless insisted on justifying ourselves and obtaining the forgiveness of our sins through our own works!

That would be as though Christ's merit, righteousness, and blood were not enough, unless we added our own filthy righteousness, stained with self-love, selfish interest, and a thousand kinds of vanity. For these works we ought to ask God for pardon rather than reward. And we would be ignoring Paul's warning to the Galatians. Deceived by false preachers, they did not believe justification through faith was sufficient in itself and wanted to be justified by keeping the Law as well.

Paul told them, "Christ is of no benefit to you who seek to be justified by the Law; you have fallen away from grace. For through the Spirit, by faith, we eagerly wait for the hope of righteousness."

If seeking righteousness and forgiveness by observing the Law—the very Law that God gave with such glory and splendor on Mount Sinai—means losing Christ and his grace, what shall we say of people who hope to justify themselves before God by their own regulations and observances? Let them compare the two and judge for themselves. If God does not grant such honor and glory to his own Law, do they think he will grant it to their rules and constitutions?

This honor belongs only to his only Son. Through the sacrifice of his suffering, Christ alone has made satisfaction for all our sins—past, present, and future—as Paul shows in the letter to the Hebrews and John in his first letter. Whenever we apply Christ's satisfaction to our souls by faith, we certainly receive the forgiveness of sins; and through his righteousness we become good and righteous before God.

Paul therefore says to the Philippians, after explaining that as far as legal righteousness was concerned, he had lived without blame: "Yet whatever was gain to me I counted as loss because of Christ. Indeed, I count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord. For his sake I have suffered the loss of everything and consider it refuse, so that I may gain Christ and be found in him—not having a righteousness of my own that comes from the Law, but the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ, the righteousness from God that rests on faith—so that I may know him."

These are most remarkable words. Every Christian ought to engrave them on the heart and pray that God will give a full understanding and appreciation of them. Paul plainly shows that anyone who truly knows Christ counts works of the Law as loss whenever they turn a person away from trusting in Christ, in whom all hope of salvation must rest, and instead lead a person to trust in himself.

To press the point even more strongly, Paul says that he counts everything as refuse in order to gain Christ and be found joined to him. By this he shows that anyone who trusts in works and seeks to be justified through them does not gain Christ and is never joined to him.

Because the whole mystery of faith rests on this truth, Paul goes further to help us understand his meaning. He insists that he rejects every outward form of justification, every righteousness grounded in obedience to the Law. Instead, he embraces the righteousness that God gives through faith to those who believe that God punished all our sins in Christ. As Paul himself says, God has made Christ our wisdom, our righteousness, our holiness, and our redemption, so that, as Scripture says, whoever boasts should boast in the Lord and not in his own works.

It is true that certain passages of Holy Scripture, if misunderstood, may appear to contradict Paul's holy teaching and to attribute justification or the forgiveness of sins to works and to love. But others have already explained these passages very well, making it clear that those who interpret them in that way have not properly understood them.

Therefore, dearest brothers and sisters, let us not follow the foolish opinion of the senseless Galatians, but the truth Paul teaches us. Let us give all the glory for our justification to the mercy of God and the merits of his Son. Through his blood, the Son has delivered us from the dominion of the Law and the tyranny of sin and death, and has brought us into God's kingdom to give us everlasting happiness.

I say that he has freed us from the Law's dominion because he has given us his Spirit, who teaches us all truth. Christ has perfectly satisfied the Law and has given that satisfaction to all who belong to him—that is, to all true Christians. Thus they may stand with confidence before God's judgment seat, clothed in Christ's righteousness and freed through him from the Law's curse.

The Law, therefore, can no longer accuse or condemn us. It can no longer stir up our corrupt passions and desires or increase sin in us. That is why Paul says that the written record standing against us has been erased by Christ and canceled on the wood of his cross.

Having set us free from the Law's dominion, our Christ has also freed us from the tyranny of sin and death. They can no longer hold us down, since Christ conquered death through his resurrection, and we who belong to him share in his victory. Therefore we can say with Paul and the prophet Hosea, "Death has been swallowed up and destroyed."

"Where, O Death, is your sting? Where, O grave, is your victory? The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the Law. But thanks be to God, who gives us victory through Jesus Christ our Lord!" He is the blessed offspring who has crushed the head of the poisonous serpent, the devil. All who believe in Christ and put their entire confidence in his grace conquer sin, death, the devil, and hell together with Christ.

He is the blessed offspring of Abraham in whom God promised to bless all nations. Otherwise each person would have had to crush that terrible serpent separately and free himself from the curse. But such a task is so great that even the combined strength of the whole world would not be enough to accomplish it.

Therefore our God, the Father of mercies, moved by compassion for our misery, has given us his only Son. He has freed us from the serpent's poison and become our blessing and our justification, if only we receive him and renounce every other claim to justification.

Let us embrace the righteousness of our Jesus Christ, dearest brothers and sisters. Let us make it our own through faith, holding firmly to the conviction that we are righteous not because of our works, but because of Christ's merits. Let us live in joy and confidence, knowing that Christ's righteousness cancels out all our unrighteousness and makes us good, righteous, and holy in God's sight.

When God sees us united to his Son through faith, he no longer regards us as children of Adam but as his own children, making us heirs together with his rightful Son of all his riches.


Chapter 4 — The Effects of Living Faith and the Union of the Soul with Christ.


This holy and living faith accomplishes so much that whoever believes Christ has taken his sins upon himself becomes like Christ and conquers sin, death, the devil, and hell. That is why the Church—that is, every faithful soul—is called Christ's bride, and Christ her bridegroom.

We know what happens in marriage: two people become one, being joined as one flesh, and the possessions of each become common to both. So the bridegroom says that the bride's dowry is his, and the bride says that her husband's house and all his wealth are hers. And so they truly are; otherwise they would not be one flesh, as Holy Scripture says.

In this same way God has joined his dearly beloved Son in marriage to the faithful soul. Although she has nothing of her own but sin, the Son of God did not disdain to take her as his beloved bride, together with her own dowry, which is sin. Because of the union established by this most holy marriage, whatever belongs to one belongs also to the other.

Christ therefore says: 'The dowry of my dear bride, the soul—that is, her sins, her violations of the Law, the wrath of God against her, the devil's bold accusations against her, the prison of hell, and all her other evils—has now passed into my hands and become my own possession. It belongs to me to deal with it as I please. So I choose to cast it into the fire of my cross and bring it to nothing.'

Seeing his Son stained with the sins of his bride, God punished him by putting him to death on the wood of the cross. Yet because he was God's dearest and most obedient Son, God raised him from death to life, gave him all authority in heaven and on earth, and seated him at his right hand.

Likewise the bride says with the greatest joy: 'The kingdoms and dominions of my beloved Bridegroom are mine. I am queen and empress of heaven and earth. My husband's riches—his holiness, innocence, righteousness, divinity, and all his virtues and powers—belong to me. Therefore I am holy, innocent, righteous, and made a partaker of divine life. There is no stain in me. I am beautiful and lovely, because my dearest Bridegroom is without stain and is beautiful and lovely. Since he is entirely mine, everything that belongs to him is mine too. And because his possessions are holy and pure, I too become holy and pure.'

Beginning with his completely innocent birth, Christ has sanctified the birth of his bride, who was conceived in sin. The innocent childhood and youth of the Bridegroom have made righteous the childhood, youth, and imperfect actions of his beloved bride. Such is the love and union between the soul of a true Christian and her Bridegroom, Christ, that their works belong to them both.

Thus, when we say, 'Christ fasted; Christ prayed and was heard by his Father; Christ raised the dead, freed people from demons, healed the sick, died, rose again, and ascended to heaven,' we may also say that the Christian has done these very things. For the works of Christ are the works of the Christian: Christ has done them all on the Christian's behalf.

We can truly say that the Christian has been nailed to the cross, buried, raised again, and taken up into heaven; has become a child of God; and has been made a partaker of the divine nature. Conversely, all the works the Christian does belong to Christ, since he claims them as his own. And because these works are imperfect, whereas he is perfect and wants nothing imperfect, he makes them perfect by his own power, so that his bride may always be joyful and satisfied and need not fear.

Although her works are deficient, they are nevertheless pleasing to God because of his Son, upon whom God continually looks. O immeasurable goodness of God! How great is the Christian's debt of gratitude to him! No human love is great enough to compare with the love of God, the beloved Bridegroom of every faithful Christian soul.

For this reason Paul says that Christ loved the Church—that is, each soul whom he loves as his bride—and gave himself for her, even to death on the cross, in order to sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water with the word, and to present her to himself as a glorious Church, without spot or wrinkle or anything like them, but holy and blameless. In other words, he makes her like himself in holiness and innocence, a true and legitimate child of God. God so loved the world, as Christ says, that he gave his only Son, so that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life. God did not send his Son into the world to condemn it, but that the world might be saved through him. Whoever believes in him is not condemned.

Someone might ask me: 'How does this divine marriage take place? How is the soul, the bride, joined to her Bridegroom, Christ? How can I be certain that my own soul is united to Christ and has become his bride? How can I confidently boast in his riches, as the bride has done above? It is easy for me to believe that others receive such honor and glory. But I cannot persuade myself that I am one of those to whom God gives so many graces. I know too well my own misery and imperfection.'

Dearest brother, I answer that your certainty rests in true and living faith, by which, as Peter says, God purifies our hearts. This faith consists in believing the gospel: the joyful news published by God throughout the world that God has exercised the full severity of his justice against Christ, punishing in him all our sins.

Whoever accepts and truly believes this good news has true faith. Such a person enjoys forgiveness of sins, is reconciled to God, becomes a child of grace instead of a child of wrath, recovers the image of God, enters his kingdom, and becomes his temple. Through this faith God joins the soul in marriage to his only Son. Faith itself is God's work and God's gift, as Paul repeatedly says.

God gives it to those whom he calls to himself, so that he may justify and glorify them and give them eternal life. Christ himself testifies: 'This is the will of him who sent me: that everyone who sees the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day.' Likewise he says: 'Just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, so that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.' To Martha he said: 'Whoever believes in me, though he die, shall live; and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die.' To the crowds of Jews he said: 'I have come as light into the world, so that whoever believes in me may not remain in darkness.'

In his letter, John says: 'In this the love of God toward us was revealed: God is love, and he sent his only Son into the world so that we might live through him. This is love—not that we loved God, but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins.'

Beyond this, God sent him to destroy our enemies. To this end he made him a sharer in our flesh and blood so that, through death, he might destroy the one who held the power of death—that is, the devil—and free all those who had been enslaved throughout their lives by the fear of death, as Paul says.

Since we have Holy Scripture's testimony to these promises, which we have discussed, and to many others scattered through its pages, we cannot doubt their truth. And since Scripture speaks in general terms, no one should doubt that what it says applies to him.

To explain this better, because the whole mystery of faith rests upon it, imagine a good and holy king who issues a public proclamation that all rebels may safely return to his kingdom, since he has pardoned them all for the merits of one of their kinsmen. Surely none of the rebels should doubt that he has really received forgiveness for his rebellion. Each should return confidently to his home and live under that holy king's protection. If he refused to return, however, he would suffer the consequences: because of his unbelief, he would die in exile, out of favor with his king.

This holy king is the Lord of heaven and earth. Because of the obedience and merits of Christ, our kinsman, he has pardoned all our rebellion. As we have already said, he has had a proclamation issued throughout the world inviting us all to return safely to his kingdom. Whoever believes this proclamation returns to the kingdom of God, from which the sin of our first parents expelled us, and is happily governed by the Spirit of God.

Whoever refuses to believe the proclamation does not enjoy this general pardon. Through unbelief he remains in exile under the devil's tyranny, living and dying in utter misery and out of favor with the King of heaven and earth. And rightly so, for we can do God no greater offense than to make him out to be a liar and deceiver, as we do when we refuse to believe his promises.

How grave is this sin of unbelief! So far as it is able, it strips God of his glory and perfection, besides bringing condemnation on the unbeliever and continually tormenting the unhappy conscience even in this life.

By contrast, whoever draws near to God with a sincere heart and full assurance of faith, believing his promises without the slightest suspicion and holding fast to the certainty of receiving everything God promises, gives glory to God. Such a person lives in continual peace and joy, always praising and thanking God for choosing him for the glory of eternal life. He possesses the surest pledge of that glory: God's own Son as his beloved Bridegroom, whose blood has filled his heart with joy.

This most holy faith produces a living hope and a steadfast confidence in God's mercy toward us. It lives and works in our hearts, enabling us to rest completely in God and leave our care to him. Knowing God's favor toward us, we no longer fear the devil, his servants, or death. Such firm and courageous trust in God's mercy opens the heart, stirs it, draws it toward God with the sweetest affections, and fills it with the most ardent love.

That is why Paul urges us to approach the throne of grace with confidence and encourages us not to throw away our confidence, which carries a great reward. This holy confidence is born in the heart through the Holy Spirit, whom we receive through faith, and it never empties the heart of divine love.

From this living power we are stirred to do good and receive such strength and inclination for it that we are entirely ready to do and endure even unbearable things for the love and glory of our gracious Father, God. Through Christ he has enriched us with such abundant grace and goodwill, transforming us from enemies into his dearly loved children.

As soon as God gives a person this true faith, he is moved by a powerful love to do good works and to bear the sweetest fruit for God and neighbor, like a healthy tree. It is as impossible for this faith to remain without such fruit as it is to set a bundle of wood on fire without its giving off light.

This is that holy faith without which no one can please God, and by which all the saints of the Old and New Testaments have been saved. Paul testifies to this in speaking of Abraham, of whom Scripture says: 'Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness.' Therefore Paul says a little earlier: 'We conclude, then, that a person is justified by faith apart from the works of the Law.'

Elsewhere he says that a remnant has been saved according to the election of grace, and that if it is by grace it is no longer by works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace. To the Galatians he says it is plain that no one is justified before God by the Law, because 'the righteous shall live by faith,' whereas the Law is not based on faith, but says that whoever does what it commands shall live by doing it.

Earlier in the same letter he says that a person cannot be justified by works of the Law, but only by faith in Jesus Christ. A little later he declares that if righteousness can come through the Law, Christ died for nothing.

To the Romans, comparing the righteousness of the Law with the righteousness of the gospel, Paul says that the former rests on doing and the latter on believing: 'If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. For with the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth one confesses unto salvation.' See how clearly Paul shows that faith makes a person righteous without the assistance of works.

And not Paul alone: the holy teachers who came after him have also affirmed and defended this most holy truth of justification by faith. Chief among them is Augustine. In On Faith and Works, On the Spirit and the Letter, Eighty-Three Different Questions, his writing to Pope Boniface, his exposition of Psalm 31, and many other places, Augustine defends this teaching. He shows that we are justified by faith without the assistance of good works, because good works are not the cause but the result of justification. He also shows that the words of James, understood correctly, do not contradict this teaching.

Origen likewise defends it in the fourth book of his commentary on Romans, affirming that Paul teaches that faith alone is sufficient for justification. A person is made righteous simply through believing, even if he has not performed any work. The thief on the cross was justified apart from works of the Law: the Lord did not ask what he had done before, nor did he wait for the man to do anything afterward. Rather, when the thief believed and made his confession, the Lord justified him and accepted him as his companion as he entered paradise.

And that woman so well known from Luke's Gospel heard Jesus say at his feet, 'Your sins are forgiven,' and shortly afterward, 'Your faith has saved you; go in peace.' Origen goes on to say that in many passages of the Gospel the Lord speaks in a way that shows faith to be the cause of a believer's salvation. Thus it is faith that justifies a person; the works of the Law contribute nothing to that justification.

Conversely, where there is no faith to justify the believer, even if a person performs the works commanded by the Law, those works have not been built upon the foundation of faith. However good they may appear, they cannot justify the one who does them, because he lacks faith—the distinguishing mark of those whom God has justified. Who, then, could boast of his own righteousness after hearing God say through the prophet, 'All our righteousness is like a filthy garment'? Therefore the only rightful boasting is in faith in the cross of Christ.

Basil, in his homily On Humility, expressly teaches that a Christian is righteous only through faith in Christ. He says: 'The apostle says, “Let the one who boasts boast in the Lord.” Christ has been made by God our wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption, so that, as it is written, whoever boasts should boast in the Lord. For the perfect and complete way of glorying in God is when a person does not exalt himself on account of his own righteousness, but recognizes that he lacks true righteousness and is justified by faith alone in Christ.'

Paul likewise boasts of rejecting his own righteousness and seeking the righteousness from God through faith in Christ. Hilary, in the ninth section of his commentary on Matthew, writes: 'The scribes were troubled that sins should be forgiven by a man, because they regarded Jesus Christ as a mere man. Yet he forgave what the Law could not forgive, because faith alone justifies.'

Ambrose, commenting on Paul's words, 'To the one who believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness according to the purpose of God's grace,' and on David's pronouncement of blessing upon 'the person to whom God credits righteousness apart from works,' writes: 'Paul says that for the one who believes in Christ, the Gentile, faith is credited as righteousness just as it was for Abraham. How, then, did the Jews imagine they could be justified by works of the Law through Abraham's justification, when Abraham was not justified by works of the Law but by faith alone? The Law, therefore, is not necessary for this, because the ungodly person is justified before God by faith alone, according to the purpose of God's grace.'

Ambrose continues that God has determined that, with the Law no longer serving this purpose, the unrighteous person needs only faith in God's grace for salvation, as David also declares. The apostle supports what he says by the prophet's example: 'Blessed is the person to whom God credits righteousness apart from works.' David means that those are blessed whom God has ordained to justify before himself, without toil or any outward observance, through faith alone. Thus he foretells the blessedness of the time when Christ would be born, as the Lord says: 'Many righteous people and prophets longed to see what you see and hear what you hear, and did not hear it.'

The same Ambrose, commenting on the first chapter of First Corinthians, states very plainly that whoever believes in Christ is justified without works and without any merit, receiving forgiveness of sins through faith alone. He affirms the same in a letter to Irenaeus: 'Let no one boast of works, for no one is justified by his own works. Rather, whoever is righteous receives righteousness as a gift, because he is justified through Christ. Faith, then, is what frees us through Christ's blood. Blessed is the one whose sin has been forgiven and whose pardon has been granted.'

Bernard also confirms this teaching in his twenty-seventh sermon on the Song of Songs. He maintains that our merits contribute nothing to justification, which must be attributed entirely to grace. Grace makes us righteous freely and delivers us from bondage to sin. He adds that Christ joins himself to the soul in marriage through faith, without any merits of our works taking part in it.

Not to prolong the quotations unnecessarily, I will bring them to a close after relating one beautiful saying of Ambrose. In his book On Jacob and the Blessed Life, he writes that Jacob had done nothing of himself to merit the birthright, yet hid himself beneath his brother's clothing. Dressed in that garment, which gave off a sweet fragrance, he came before his father and received the blessing under another person's identity. In the same way, if we wish to be accepted as righteous in God's sight, we must clothe ourselves by faith in the righteousness of Christ and hide ourselves beneath the precious purity of our elder Brother.

Surely this is true. If we appear before God without the righteousness of Christ as our clothing, we will all be judged unrighteous and deserving of punishment. But if God sees us adorned in Christ's righteousness, he will without doubt accept us as righteous, holy, and worthy of eternal life.

How presumptuous, then, are those who expect to obtain justification by observing God's commandments! All those commandments are summed up in loving God with all the heart, all the soul, and all the strength, and loving our neighbor as ourselves. Who is so arrogant and foolish as to imagine he perfectly keeps these two commandments? Who cannot see that God's Law demands perfect love and therefore condemns every imperfection?

Let everyone examine even those actions he considers good. He will find that they should more properly be called violations of God's holy Law, because they are impure and imperfect. This is why David cries, 'Do not enter into judgment with your servant, for no one living will be justified in your sight.' Solomon asks, 'Who can say, “My heart is clean”?' Job exclaims, 'What is a human being, that he should be spotless, or one born of a woman, that he should appear righteous? Even among God's holy ones no one is unchangeable, and the heavens are not pure in his sight. How much less pure is the corrupt and useless person who drinks wrongdoing like water!'

John says, 'If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves.' And the Lord taught us to pray every time, 'Forgive us our debts.' How foolish, therefore, are those who make a business of their works, imagining that by them they can save not only themselves but their neighbors as well. Has not the Lord said, 'When you have done everything commanded you, say, “We are unprofitable servants; we have done only what we were obliged to do”'? Even if we kept God's Law perfectly, we would still have to judge and call ourselves unprofitable servants.

Since every person falls so far short of keeping it perfectly, how could anyone dare boast that he had accumulated so many merits beyond what was required that he could give some away to others? Returning to our subject, let the arrogant sinner who performs works praised by the world, and therefore claims to be justified before God, consider this: everything that comes from an impure and unclean heart is itself unclean and impure, and so cannot please God or justify the person who does it.

The heart must first be purified if our works are to please God. Its purification comes through faith, as the Holy Spirit declares through Paul. We must not say, then, that an unrighteous and sinful person becomes righteous, good, and pleasing to God through his works. Rather, faith cleanses our hearts of every sin, makes us good and righteous and pleasing to God, and consequently causes even our imperfect and deficient works to be pleasing to his Majesty.

For when faith has made us God's children, he looks upon our works as a compassionate Father rather than a severe judge. He has pity on our weakness and sees us as members of his firstborn Son, whose righteousness and perfection supply what is lacking in our uncleanness and imperfection. Hidden beneath Christ's purity and innocence, those faults are not counted against us or brought before God's judgment.

So at the final judgment Christ will praise and approve works that spring from genuine faith, although considered in themselves those works are impure and imperfect. He will approve them as the fruit and evidence of the faith by which we are saved. In loving Christ's brothers and sisters, we will show openly that we too have been faithful people and brothers and sisters of Christ. Through faith we will enter into full possession of the eternal kingdom God prepared for us from the creation of the world, not because of our merits but because of his mercy.

Out of that mercy he chose us, called us to the grace of the gospel, and justified us so that we might share in eternal glory with his only Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our sanctification and our righteousness. This does not belong to those who refuse to confess that Christ's righteousness is sufficient in itself to make a person righteous and acceptable to God. In his fatherly kindness God offers and gives us Christ and his righteousness without any merit of our works.

What could a human being possibly do to deserve so great a gift and treasure as Christ? This treasure is given solely by God's grace, favor, and mercy. Faith alone receives the gift and enables us to enjoy the forgiveness of sins.

Thus, when Paul and the teachers of the Church say that faith alone justifies apart from works, they mean that faith alone lets us enjoy God's universal proclamation of pardon and receive Christ. As Paul says, Christ dwells in our hearts through faith. Christ has overcome the terrors of our consciences, satisfied divine justice for our sins, quenched God's wrath against us and the flames of hell into which our natural and acquired corruption had plunged us, and destroyed the demons with all their power and tyranny.

Not even all the works of all humanity together could achieve these things. Such glory and power are reserved for the Son of God alone, our blessed Christ, who is stronger than every power in heaven, on earth, and in hell. He gives himself and all his merits to those who despair of themselves and place their entire hope of salvation in him and in his merits.

Let no one, hearing that faith alone justifies without works, fall into the mistake made by false Christians who twist everything into an excuse for fleshly living. They imagine true faith is merely believing the story of Jesus Christ in the way one believes stories about Caesar or Alexander. Such belief is only historical faith, founded on a human report or written narrative, lightly impressed upon the mind by habit. It is comparable to the belief of those who accept the stories of the Qur'an for similar reasons.

Faith of this kind is merely a human opinion. It changes nothing in the human heart, does not warm it with God's love, and produces neither the good works that spring from faith nor a new life. Because they mistake this for Christian faith, some wrongly say, contrary to Scripture and the holy teachers of the Church, that faith alone does not justify and must be supplemented with works.

I answer that such empty historical belief, together with any works added to it, not only fails to justify but leads its possessors into the depths of hell. They resemble those who had no oil in their lamps—that is, no living faith in their hearts. Faith that justifies is God's own work within us. Through it our old self is crucified, we are transformed in Christ, become a new creation, and become God's beloved children.

This divine faith joins us to Christ's death and resurrection. Consequently it puts our flesh to death, along with its passions and desires. Knowing through faith that we have died with Christ, we die to ourselves and to the world. We understand that those who have died with Christ ought to put to death their earthly members—the sinful affections of the soul and appetites of the flesh. Knowing that we have been raised with Christ, we seek to live a spiritual and holy life like the life we shall live in heaven after the final resurrection.

By giving us the pardon announced in the gospel, this most holy faith brings us into God's kingdom, gives peace to the conscience, and sustains us in continual holy and spiritual joy. The same faith unites us with God, makes him dwell in our hearts, and clothes our souls with himself. Consequently his Spirit moves us toward the very things Christ pursued when he walked among humanity: humility, gentleness, obedience to God, love, and every other virtue through which we recover God's image.

It is therefore right for Christ to attribute blessedness to this God-given faith. That blessedness cannot exist without good works and holiness. And how could a Christian fail to be holy when Christ himself becomes his sanctification through faith? Through faith, then, we are righteous and holy. This is why Paul so often calls 'saints' those whom we call 'Christians.' If they do not have Christ's Spirit they do not belong to Christ and therefore are not Christians. If they have Christ's Spirit ruling and guiding them, we must not suppose that learning they are justified by faith alone will make them lazy in good works.

For Christ's Spirit is the Spirit of love, and love cannot remain idle or cease doing good. Indeed, to speak truly, a person cannot perform genuinely good works until he recognizes that he has been justified by faith. Before then he works more to justify himself than out of love for God or concern for God's glory. Thus self-love and self-interest stain his actions. But when a person knows he has been justified through Christ's merits and righteousness and receives that righteousness through faith, he acts solely from love of God and Christ, not from self-interest or to secure his own justification.

Therefore the true Christian, who regards himself as righteous through Christ's righteousness, does not ask whether good works are commanded or not. Driven by the irresistible force of divine love, he willingly offers himself for holy and Christian deeds and never ceases doing good.

Anyone who does not experience the marvelous effects of God-given faith that we have described should recognize that he does not yet possess Christian faith and should urgently pray to God to give it to him, saying, 'Lord, help my unbelief!' Nor should someone, on hearing that faith alone justifies, deceive himself by saying, 'Why should I trouble myself with good works? Faith is all I need to get to paradise.'

To this I reply: Yes, faith alone brings a person to paradise; but remember that even demons believe—and tremble—as James says. Do you expect to go to paradise with them? Brother, your false conclusion shows the depth of your error. You imagine you have the faith that justifies, but you do not. You say, 'I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing,' without realizing that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked.

I counsel you to buy from God gold refined by fire—that is, true faith burning with good works—so that you may become rich. Clothe yourself in white garments, Christ's innocence, so that the shame of your nakedness, the ugliness of your sins, may not be seen.

The faith that justifies is like a flame, which cannot help giving light. The flame itself burns the wood without help from the light, but it can never exist without light. So faith alone extinguishes and burns away sins without help from works; nevertheless, this faith cannot exist without good works. If we saw a flame that gave no light, we would know it was only a painted picture, not real fire. Likewise, if we see no light of good works in a person, that is a sign that he does not possess true God-given faith, which God grants to his chosen people to justify and glorify them.

I am convinced this is what James means when he says, 'Show me your faith by your works, and I will show you my faith by my works.' Someone who pursues ambition and the pleasures of the world, however much he claims to believe, does not truly believe, since his life displays none of faith's effects.

We may also compare this most holy justifying faith to the divine nature present in Jesus Christ. Although he was truly human, and without sin, he did wonderful things: healing the sick, giving sight to the blind, walking on water, and raising the dead. But those miracles did not make Christ God. Before performing any of them he was already God, the rightful and only Son of God. He did not need to work miracles in order to become God; he performed them because he was God. The miracles did not make him divine; they showed that he truly was divine.

Similarly, true and living faith is a divine gift dwelling within the Christian. The Christian does marvelous things and never grows weary of good works. Yet those works do not make him a Christian—that is, righteous, good, holy, and greatly loved by God. He did not need to perform them to become such a person. Rather, because he is already a Christian through faith, just as Christ the man was God through his divine nature, he performs these good works. The works do not make him righteous and good; they demonstrate that he is righteous and good. Thus, just as Christ's divinity was the cause of his miracles, faith working through love is the cause of the Christian's good works.

People said of Christ, 'He has performed this miracle and that one.' Those miracles glorified God and brought great honor to Christ in his humanity. Having obeyed as far as death, Christ was rewarded by God in his resurrection with all authority in heaven and on earth—authority which, as man, he had not previously exercised in this way. His humanity shares in this dignity because of its union with the divine Word. So too, because of faith's union with the soul, what belongs to one is credited to the other.

Therefore Scripture sometimes promises eternal life to the Christian in connection with his good works, since they are the fruit and testimony of living faith and flow from it as light flows from fire, as we have already said. This most holy faith embraces Christ and joins him to the soul. Faith, Christ, and the soul are so united that what Christ merits is shared with the soul. It is for this reason Augustine says that God crowns his own gifts in us.

Christ himself bears witness to this union of the soul with him through faith. In John's Gospel he prays to the Father for his apostles and for those who would believe in him through their words: 'I do not pray for these alone, but also for those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me and I am in you; that they too may be one in us, so that the world may believe you sent me. The glory you gave me I have given them, so that they may be one, as we are one.'

By believing the apostles' message, which proclaims Christ dead for our sins and raised for our justification, we become one with Christ. And since Christ is one with God, through Christ we also become one with God. O astonishing glory of the Christian! Through faith he is allowed to possess those unspeakable things into which angels long to look.

From what has been said, the difference between our teaching and that of those who defend justification through both faith and works becomes plain. We agree on this: we too insist upon good works, affirming that justifying faith cannot exist without them. We say that those justified through faith do the works that can rightly be called good.

But we differ in saying that faith justifies without the assistance of works. The reason is simple: through faith we clothe ourselves with Christ, taking his righteousness and holiness as our own. Since Christ's righteousness is truly given to us by faith, it would be ungrateful, blind, and impious to suppose that without our works it could not be sufficient to make us righteous and acceptable before God.

We say with the apostle: 'If the blood of bulls and goats and the ashes of a heifer sprinkled upon those who were defiled sanctified them for the cleansing of the flesh, how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, cleanse our consciences from dead works to serve the living God?'

Let the devout Christian judge which of these two teachings is truer, holier, and more worthy of proclamation: ours, which magnifies the benefit Christ has given and humbles human pride, which would exalt its works against Christ's glory; or the other, which says faith does not justify of itself, obscures Christ's glory and benefit, and exalts the pride of humanity, which cannot bear to be justified freely through Jesus Christ our Lord.

Some will object, 'Surely saying that a person becomes righteous before God through good works is a powerful incentive to do them!' I reply that we too confess that good works are pleasing to God and that, out of his sheer generosity, he will reward them in paradise. Yet, as Augustine also says, the works that are truly good are those performed by people justified through faith. If the tree is not good, it cannot produce good fruit.

Moreover, those justified through faith know they are righteous because of God's righteousness accomplished in Christ. They do not bargain with God by their works, attempting to purchase justification from him. Instead, set aflame by God's love and eager to glorify Christ, who has justified them by giving them all his merits and riches, they strive with all diligence to do God's will, fighting courageously against self-love, the world, and the devil.

When they fall through the weakness of the flesh, they rise again more eager to do good and more in love with their God. They consider that their sins are not counted against them, because they are joined to Christ, who made satisfaction on the cross for all his members and always intercedes for them before the eternal Father. For love of his only Son, the Father continually looks on them with a kindly face, rules and defends them as dearly loved children, and finally gives them the inheritance of the world, conforming them to the glorious image of Christ.

These loving motives are what move true Christians to good works. Considering that through faith they have become children of God and partakers of the divine nature, they are prompted by the Holy Spirit dwelling within them to live in a manner worthy of children of so great a Lord. They would be ashamed not to honor their heavenly nobility. Therefore they devote themselves to imitating their firstborn Brother, Jesus Christ: living in profound humility and gentleness, seeking God's glory in everything, laying down their lives for their brothers and sisters, doing good to their enemies, and glorying in disgrace and in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ.

With Zechariah they say, 'We have been delivered from the hands of our enemies so that we may serve God without fear, in holiness and righteousness before him, all the days of our lives.' With Paul they say, 'The grace of the Lord has appeared, teaching us to renounce all ungodliness and worldly desires and to live soberly, righteously, and godly in this present age, as we await the blessed hope and the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior.'

These and other similar thoughts, desires, and affections are brought forth by God-given faith in those whom he has justified. Anyone who feels none of these divine affections and effects in his heart, even in some degree, but is devoted to the flesh and the world, should be certain that he does not yet possess justifying faith and is not a member of Christ. He lacks the Spirit of Christ, and therefore does not belong to Christ; whoever does not belong to Christ is not a Christian.

Let human cleverness, then, stop waging war against the righteousness that comes through this most holy faith. Let us give all the glory for our justification to Christ's merits, with which we clothe ourselves through faith.


Chapter 5 — How the Christian Clothes Himself with Christ.


Although what we have already said makes it quite clear how the Christian clothes himself with Christ, we will speak a little more about it. We know that no devout Christian could ever find a discussion of Christ and his gifts too long or tiresome, even if it were repeated a thousand times.

I say that through faith a Christian knows Christ is his own, together with all his righteousness, holiness, and innocence. Just as someone puts on a beautiful and precious garment when preparing to appear before a great lord, so the Christian, adorned and covered with Christ's innocence and all his perfections, appears before God, the Lord of the universe. He trusts in Christ's merits as completely as if he himself had earned and accomplished everything Christ has earned and accomplished.

Faith assuredly makes us possess Christ and everything that belongs to him as truly as each of us possesses his own clothing. To clothe ourselves with Christ, then, means to hold firmly that Christ is ours—as he truly is if we believe it—and to believe that, dressed in this heavenly garment, we are welcomed and accepted in God's sight.

For it is absolutely certain that God, as our gracious Father, has given us his Son. He desires that all his Son's righteousness, and everything he is, can do, and has done, should become ours. Thus we may boast in these gifts as confidently as if we had achieved and earned them by our own strength. Whoever believes this will assuredly discover that what he believes is true, as we have already shown.

The Christian, therefore, should have firm faith and confidence that all Christ's gifts, graces, and riches belong to him. God has given us Christ; how could he fail to give us everything with him? Since this is true, the Christian may truthfully say: 'I am God's child, Christ is my brother, I am lord of heaven and earth, of hell and death and the Law. For the Law can neither accuse nor curse me, since my Christ's righteousness has become mine.'

Faith alone gives a person the name 'Christian' and clothes him with Christ, as we have said. This is truly a great mystery, containing the marvelous and unheard-of works of the great God. No human heart can receive these things unless God transforms it by his grace, as he promised through Ezekiel: 'I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you. I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh.'

Whoever does not believe in this way—that Christ is his, together with all the good things Christ possesses—cannot be called a true Christian. He can never have a peaceful and joyful conscience or a heart willing and ready to do good. He will easily stop doing good works; indeed, he will never perform works that are truly good.

Only this faith and confidence in Christ's merits makes people genuine Christians: courageous, joyful, delighted with God, in love with him, ready for good works, possessors of the kingdom of God, and his dearly beloved children, in whom the Holy Spirit truly dwells.

What heart could be so mean, low, and cold that, upon considering the immeasurable greatness of God's gift in giving us his beloved Son with all his perfections, it would not burn with the most fervent desire to resemble him in doing good? The Father has also given him to us as our example. We must constantly look to Christ and shape our lives into a portrait of his, for, as Peter says, 'Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example, so that we should follow in his footsteps.'

From this consideration comes another way of clothing ourselves with Christ, which we may call imitation. The Christian must govern his whole life by Christ's example, being conformed to him in every thought, word, and deed, leaving behind his former evil life and clothing himself with the new life of Christ. Paul therefore says: 'Let us cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light. Let us live not in feasting and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and lust, not in quarrelling. Instead, put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to satisfy its desires.'

The true Christian, being in love with Christ, says within himself: 'Christ did not need me, yet he redeemed me with his own blood and became poor to make me rich. In the same way I wish to give my possessions and my own life for the love and salvation of my neighbor. As I have clothed myself with Christ through the love he has shown me, so I want my neighbor in Christ, through the love I bear him for Christ's sake, to be clothed with me and with everything I possess.'

Anyone who does not act in this way is not yet a true Christian. No one should say, 'I love Christ,' if he does not love Christ's members and brothers and sisters. If we do not love our neighbor, for whose sake Christ shed his blood, we cannot truthfully say we love Christ. He, though equal with God, was obedient to the Father even to death on the cross. He loved and redeemed us by giving himself to us with all his works and all he possesses.

In the same way, we who are rich and overflowing with Christ's goodness must obey God and offer and give our works, everything we possess, and our very selves to our neighbors and brothers and sisters in Christ. We must serve them in every need, becoming almost another Christ to them.

Just as Christ was humble, gentle, and utterly removed from quarrels, so we must devote ourselves to humility and gentleness, avoiding every fight and dispute, whether expressed in words and arguments or in deeds. As Christ endured all the world's persecution and shame for God's glory, so we must joyfully endure the shame and persecution that false Christians inflict on those who wish to live godly lives in Christ. Christ laid down his life for his enemies and prayed for them on the cross. We likewise must continually pray for our enemies and willingly lay down our lives for their salvation.

This is what Peter means by following Christ's footsteps. When we recognize Christ as ours, together with all his riches—which is to clothe ourselves with Christ and become clean and pure from every stain—nothing remains for us but to glorify God by imitating Christ and doing for our brothers and sisters what Christ has done for us. We know especially from Christ's own words that everything we do for his brothers and sisters he accepts as something done for him.

Since true Christians are members of Christ, we cannot do good or evil to them without doing good or evil to Christ himself, who rejoices and suffers in his members. Therefore, just as Christ is our garment through faith, we should become a garment to our brothers and sisters through love. We should care for them with the same care we give our own bodies, for they are true members of the same body, whose head is Jesus Christ.

This is the divine love and charity that arises from sincere faith, which God breathes into his chosen people. Paul says that this faith 'works through love.' But the life of Christ, which we are to imitate and put on, was a continual cross filled with troubles, shame, and persecution. If we wish to conform ourselves to his life, we must continually carry the cross, as he said: 'If anyone wishes to follow me, let him deny himself, take up his cross daily, and follow me.'

The chief reason for this cross is that God wishes by this discipline to put to death the passions of our souls and the desires of our flesh. In this way we may experience in ourselves the perfection Christ has already secured for us by joining us to himself. God also wants our faith, refined like gold in the furnace of affliction, to shine for his praise. And he wishes our weakness to display his power—a power the world unwillingly recognizes when our frailty becomes strong under suffering and persecution and grows firmer and more steadfast the more it is beaten down and oppressed.

Paul therefore says: 'We have this treasure in earthen vessels, so that the surpassing greatness of the power may be God's and not ours. We are afflicted in every way, but not crushed; in need, but not utterly without resources; persecuted, but not abandoned; struck down, but not destroyed. We always carry in our bodies the dying of the Lord Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in our bodies.'

Seeing that Christ and his dearly loved disciples glorified God through their afflictions, let us embrace ours joyfully, saying with Paul: 'May I never boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ!'

Let us conduct ourselves so that the world, however unwillingly, may recognize and see the wonderful effects God produces in people who sincerely embrace the grace of the gospel. Let worldly people see with what calmness of soul true Christians endure the loss of their possessions, the death of their children, disgrace, bodily sickness, and persecution from false Christians. Let them see that these believers worship God in spirit and truth, receiving everything that happens to them from his hand, and regarding everything he does as good, just, and holy. In prosperity and hardship alike, they praise and thank him as their gracious and loving Father. They recognize suffering—especially suffering for the gospel and in imitation of Christ—as a great gift from God.

They know that suffering produces patience, patience produces tested character, tested character produces hope, and hope does not disappoint. I say that patience produces tested character because God has promised help in trials to those who trust him. We learn through experience that he keeps this promise as we remain strong and steadfast, sustained by his hand, when our own strength could never have sustained us.

Through patient endurance we discover by experience that the Lord gives the help he has promised in our times of need, and our hope is strengthened. It would be great ingratitude not to expect in the future the help and favor we have already found so certain and dependable in the past.

But why use so many words? It should be enough for us to know that, through afflictions, true Christians put on the image of Christ crucified. If we bear that image willingly, we shall afterward put on the image of Christ glorified. Just as Christ's sufferings abound, so our comfort will abound through Christ. And if we endure, we shall also reign with him.


Chapter 6 — Remedies Against Distrust and Unbelief.


Because the devil and human reason are always trying to strip us of the most holy faith by which we believe that all our sins have been punished in Christ, and that through his most precious blood we have been reconciled to God, Christians must always have their weapons ready to defend themselves against this evil temptation, which seeks to deprive the soul of its life.

Among these weapons I judge the most powerful to be prayer, frequent participation in Holy Communion, and remembrance of baptism and predestination. In prayer we shall say with the father of the afflicted boy, “Lord, help our unbelief!” And we shall say with the apostles, “Lord, increase our faith!” If a constant longing to grow in faith, hope, and love reigns within us, we shall indeed pray without ceasing, as Paul commands, for prayer is nothing other than a fervent desire resting on God.

By remembering our baptism we shall strengthen our assurance that we have peace with God. Peter says that Noah’s ark was a figure of baptism. Just as Noah, believing God’s promises, was saved from the flood in the ark, so through faith we are saved from God’s wrath in baptism. This faith rests on Christ’s word: “Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved.”

And rightly so, for in baptism we put on Christ, as Paul affirms. Consequently, we become sharers in his righteousness and all his gifts. Beneath this most precious garment the sins committed through our frailty are covered and are not counted against us by God. As Paul says, we receive the blessing described in the psalm: “Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven and whose sins are covered! Blessed is the person against whom the Lord does not count sin!”

But Christians must beware of using these words as permission to sin. This teaching is not for those who boast of the Christian name and confess Christ with their words while denying him by their deeds. It is for true Christians who, although they fight courageously against the flesh, the world, and the devil, still fall every day and must continually pray, “Forgive us our debts.”

We speak to these people to comfort and uphold them, so that they do not fall into despair, as if Christ’s blood did not cleanse us from every sin or as if he were not the advocate and atoning sacrifice for his own members.

Therefore, when we are tempted to doubt the forgiveness of our sins and our conscience begins to be troubled, let us immediately turn, armed with faith, to the precious blood of Jesus Christ. It was shed for us upon the altar of the cross; and at the Last Supper it was offered to believers under the veil of the most holy sacrament. Christ instituted this sacrament so that we might remember his death and, by this visible sign, find assurance for our afflicted consciences that we have been reconciled with God.

Our blessed Christ made his testament when he said, “This is my body, given for you; and this is my blood of the new covenant, shed for many for the forgiveness of sins.” We know that a testament, as Paul says, even when it is only a human agreement, is not disregarded or added to after it has been confirmed. And a testament does not take effect before the testator dies, but becomes binding after his death.

Christ’s testament promises forgiveness of sins, his own favor and his Father’s, mercy, and eternal life. So that this testament might be established, he confirmed it with his precious blood and his own death.

Paul accordingly says that Christ is the mediator of the new testament, so that, by means of a death redeeming the transgressions committed under the first covenant, those who have been called might receive the promise of the eternal inheritance. Wherever there is a testament, the testator’s death must take place, for a testament is confirmed when someone has died and does not take effect while the testator is living.

Thus by Christ’s death we may be completely certain that his testament stands: all our wrongdoings are forgiven and we have been made heirs of eternal life. As a sign and pledge of this, he has left us this divine sacrament in place of a seal. It not only gives our souls sure confidence of eternal salvation, but also assures us of the immortality of our bodies. Even now our flesh receives life from his immortal flesh, and in a certain way becomes a participant in its immortality.

Whoever partakes of this divine flesh with faith will never perish. But for one who partakes without faith it becomes a deadly poison. Just as bodily food becomes corrupted and harmful when it encounters a stomach filled with unhealthy humors, so this spiritual food, when it encounters a soul corrupted by malice and unbelief, brings that soul into greater ruin. The fault is not in the food itself, but in the one receiving it, because nothing is pure to the impure and unbelieving, even when it has been sanctified by the Lord’s blessing.

For Paul says: “Whoever eats this bread or drinks this cup unworthily will be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. Such a person eats and drinks judgment on himself, because he does not discern the Lord’s body.”

A person fails to discern the Lord’s body when he takes part in the Lord’s Supper without faith or love. He does not believe that this body is his life and the cleansing of all his sins. He makes Christ out to be a liar, tramples underfoot the Son of God, treats the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified as an unholy thing, and insults the Spirit of grace. God will punish such unbelief and wicked hypocrisy most severely.

For this man has not placed his confidence for justification in the suffering of Christ. Nevertheless, by receiving the holy sacrament he professes that he trusts in nothing else. He thus accuses himself, bears witness against his own wrongdoing, and pronounces sentence upon himself, rejecting the eternal life that God promises him in this holy sacrament.

Therefore, when Christians feel that their enemies are about to overcome them—that is, when they doubt whether they have received forgiveness of sins through Christ, whether they can withstand the devil’s temptations, and when the accusations of an uncertain conscience prevail against them, so that they fear hell will swallow them up and death will conquer and destroy them forever under God’s wrath—when they feel such distress, let them come to this holy sacrament with courage and confidence and receive it reverently.

Let them speak within their hearts and answer their enemies in this way:

“I confess that because of my sins I deserve a thousand hells and eternal death. But this holy sacrament, which I now receive, assures me of the forgiveness of all my wrongdoings and of my reconciliation with God.

“If I look at my works, I cannot fail to recognize myself as a sinner under condemnation. My conscience will never find rest if I believe that my sins are forgiven because of the works I perform. But if I look to God’s promises and covenant, in which he promises me the forgiveness of sins through Christ’s blood, I am as certain that I have obtained forgiveness and his grace as I am certain that the One who promised and made the covenant can neither lie nor deceive.

“Through this steadfast faith I am made righteous. This is the righteousness of Christ by which I am saved and my conscience is quieted.

“Did he not give his most innocent body into the hands of sinners for our sins? Did he not pour out his blood to cleanse all my wrongdoings? Why, then, are you cast down, O my soul? Put your trust in the Lord, who loves you so deeply that, to free you from eternal death, he was willing that his only Son should die. That Son took our poverty upon himself to give us his riches; he took our weakness to strengthen us with his might; he became mortal to make us immortal; he descended to earth so that we might ascend to heaven; he became the Son of Man with us so that we might become children of God with him.

“Who, then, will bring an accusation against us? God is the one who justifies us. Who will condemn us? Christ died for us—and more than that, he rose again, sits at the right hand of God, and intercedes for us.

“Leave off your weeping and sighing, O my soul! Bless the Lord, O my soul, and let everything within me bless his holy name. Bless the Lord, O my soul, and never forget all his benefits! He forgives all your wrongdoings, heals all your diseases, rescues your life from death, and crowns you with mercy and compassion. The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.

“He will not contend forever or keep his anger eternally. He has not dealt with us according to our sins or repaid us according to our wrongdoings. As high as heaven is above the earth, so great is his mercy toward those who fear him. As far as east is from west, so far has he removed our transgressions from us. As a father has compassion on his child, so the Lord has had compassion on us by giving us his only Son.”

With this faith, with these thanksgivings, and with these and similar thoughts, we ought to receive the sacrament of the body and blood of Jesus Christ our Lord. In this way fear is driven out of the soul, love increases, faith is strengthened, conscience grows calm, and the tongue never tires of praising God and giving him endless thanks for so great a benefit. This is the power, the effectiveness, and the soul’s only ground of confidence.

This is the rock upon which conscience may be built without fear of any storm—not even the gates of hell, God’s wrath, the Law, sin, death, demons, or anything else.

Because the whole essence of the Mass is found in this divine sacrament, whenever Christians are present at it they ought always to fix the eyes of their minds upon the suffering of our most gracious Lord. On one side they should contemplate him on the cross, carrying all our sins; on the other, they should contemplate God punishing those sins by scourging his beloved Son in our place.

How blessed is the person who shuts his eyes to every other spectacle and neither wishes to see nor hear anything but Jesus Christ crucified, in whom all the graces and all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden! Blessed, I say, is the person who continually nourishes his mind on such divine food and refreshes his soul with so sweet and saving a drink of the love of God!

But before I bring this discussion to an end, I wish to remind Christians that Augustine often calls this divine sacrament “the bond of love” and “the mystery of unity.” He says, “Whoever receives the mystery of unity and does not preserve the bond of peace receives the mystery not for his benefit, but as a witness against himself.”

We should therefore know that the Lord instituted this sacrament not only to assure us of the forgiveness of sins, but also to kindle peace, union, and brotherly love in us. In this sacrament the Lord makes us partakers of his body in such a way that he becomes one with us, and we with him. Because he has only one body, of which he makes all of us partakers, all of us must also become one body through that participation.

The bread of the sacrament represents this unity. Just as it is made from many grains mingled and blended together so completely that one cannot distinguish one from another, so we ought to be joined in such harmony of spirit that not even the smallest division arises among us.

Paul teaches this when he says: “The cup of blessing which we bless—is it not a participation in the blood of Christ? And the bread we break—is it not a participation in the body of Christ? Though we are many, we are one bread and one body, because we all share in the one bread.”

Therefore, whenever we receive Holy Communion, let us consider that we are all incorporated into Christ. We are all members of one body, members of Christ himself. We cannot offend, disgrace, or despise any brother or sister without at the same time offending, disgracing, or despising Jesus Christ. We cannot quarrel with our brothers and sisters without quarrelling with Christ. We cannot love Christ without loving him in our brothers and sisters.

We should care for our brothers and sisters, who are members of our body, as carefully as we care for our own bodies. Just as no part of our body feels pain without every other part being affected, so we ought not to allow a brother or sister to suffer without being moved to compassion ourselves. Such are the thoughts with which we should prepare ourselves for this great sacrament, stirring up ardent love for our neighbors.

For what could encourage us more strongly to mutual love than to see that Christ, in giving himself to us, not only calls us to give ourselves to one another, but also, by becoming common to us all, makes us all one in him? We should therefore desire and seek that we may have one soul, one heart, and one voice, united and in agreement in our thoughts, words, and deeds.

Let every Christian remember that each time we receive this most holy sacrament we bind ourselves to every duty of love. We ought neither to offend our brothers and sisters in anything nor to neglect anything we can do to help them in their needs.

If some come to the Lord’s heavenly table while divided and estranged from their brothers and sisters, let them know that they eat unworthily. They are guilty of the body and blood of the Lord and eat and drink judgment upon themselves. For by hating their brothers and sisters, who are members of Christ, they are tearing and dividing Christ’s body as far as lies within their power. Though they have no share in Christ, by receiving Holy Communion they nevertheless profess to believe that their whole salvation consists in participation in and union with him.

Let us therefore approach this heavenly bread to remember the Lord’s suffering; to sustain and strengthen, through that remembrance, our faith and our certainty of the forgiveness of sins; to stir our hearts and tongues to praise and proclaim our God’s infinite goodness; and finally to nourish our mutual love and bear witness to it before one another because of the intimate union we all share in the body of Jesus Christ our Lord.

Besides prayer, remembrance of baptism, and frequent participation in Holy Communion, another excellent remedy against distrust and the fear that opposes Christian love is the remembrance of our predestination and election to eternal life. That assurance is founded on the word of God, the sword of the Holy Spirit, with which we can overcome our enemies.

“Rejoice,” says the Lord, “because your names are written in heaven.” There is no greater joy in this present life, nor anything that more deeply comforts a Christian who is troubled, tempted, or fallen into some sin, than remembering his predestination and being assured that he belongs to those whose names are written in the book of life, whom God has chosen to be conformed to the image of Christ.

What unspeakable comfort belongs to a person with such faith, who continually turns this sweet truth of predestination over in his heart! Although he may fall, God his Father, who has destined him for eternal life, always supports him with his hand. Such a person continually says within himself, “If God has chosen and predestined me to share the glory of his children, who can hinder it?” As Paul says, “If God is for us, who can be against us?”

Indeed, to accomplish our predestination God sent his beloved Son, who is the surest pledge that we who have received the grace of the gospel are children of God, chosen for eternal life. This holy predestination preserves the true Christian in continual spiritual joy, increases his desire for good works, sets his heart on fire with love for God, and makes him an enemy of the world and of sin.

Who could be so hard and unfeeling that, knowing God has by his mercy made him his child from eternity, he would not burn with divine love? Who could be so timid and small-hearted that he would not count all the pleasures, honors, and riches of the world as worthless mud, knowing that God has made him a citizen of heaven?

These are the people who truly worship God in spirit and truth, receiving both prosperity and adversity from the hand of God their Father and always praising and thanking him as a loving, just, and holy Father in everything he does. Because they love their God and are armed with the knowledge of their predestination, they fear neither death, sin, the devil, nor hell. They know nothing of God’s wrath against them, for they see in him nothing but a Father’s love and kindness toward them.

If they suffer tribulations, they accept them as favors from their God and cry out with Paul: “Who will separate us from the love of God? Tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, or the sword? As it is written, ‘For your sake we are put to death all day long; we are counted as sheep for slaughter.’ Yet in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.”

With good reason, then, John says that true Christians know they shall be saved and glorified and that through this confidence they purify themselves as Christ is pure. When Paul urges his disciples to lead a godly and holy life, he regularly reminds them of their election and predestination, knowing that nothing more effectively stirs up love for God and eagerness for good works in truly Christian hearts.

For the same reason our blessed Christ spoke openly of this holy predestination, knowing how important its knowledge is for building up the elect.

But perhaps you will say to me: “I understand that those whose names are written in heaven have reason to live in continual joy and glorify God in word and deed. But I do not know whether I belong to that number. I therefore live in continual fear, especially because I know how weak and easily led into sin I am. I cannot defend myself against its power so completely that I am not overcome each day. And when I see myself repeatedly afflicted and harassed by various troubles, I almost seem to see with my own eyes the wrath of God punishing me.”

In answer to these doubts, dearest brother or sister, I tell you to be certain that these are temptations from the devil. He seeks by every means to strip us of faith and of the confidence born from faith, which assures us of God’s goodwill.

The devil seeks to strip the Christian soul of this precious garment because he knows that no one is truly faithful who does not believe God’s words. God promises forgiveness of all sins and his peace to everyone who receives the grace of the gospel. Anyone who, because of these promises, is not fully persuaded that God is his kind and forgiving Father, and who does not confidently expect from him the inheritance of the heavenly kingdom, does not truly believe: such a person is completely unworthy of God’s grace.

Therefore Paul says that we are God’s house, provided we hold fast our confidence and the glory of our hope firmly to the end. Elsewhere he urges us not to cast away our confidence, which has a great reward.

So then, dearest brother or sister, let us strive diligently to do God’s will as good children, and avoid sin as much as we can. Yet if we fall through our frailty, let us not therefore believe that we are vessels of wrath or have been abandoned by the Holy Spirit. We have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous, who is the atoning sacrifice for our sins.

Let us remember Augustine’s saying that no holy and righteous person is without sin, yet such a person does not cease to be holy and righteous so long as the heart continues to cling to holiness. Therefore, if we are afflicted and troubled, let us not suppose that God sends us suffering because he is our enemy, but rather because he is our most merciful Father. As Solomon says, “The Lord disciplines the one he loves and chastens every child he receives.”

Having received the grace of the gospel, through which a person is accepted by God as his child, we must not doubt God’s grace and goodwill. When we find delight in God’s words and in imitating Christ’s life, we should be assured that we are children of God and temples of the Holy Spirit. Such things cannot be accomplished by human wisdom; they are gifts of the Holy Spirit, who lives in us through faith. He is like a seal authenticating and sealing in our hearts those divine promises whose certainty he has already impressed upon our minds. God has given him as a pledge to establish and confirm them.


All of us who believe in Jesus Christ are children of God, as Paul says. And because we are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba, Father!” He also tells the Romans, “All who are led by the Spirit of God are children of God. You did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, ‘Abba, Father!’ The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are God’s children; and if children, then heirs as well.”

Notice that in both passages Paul speaks quite plainly, not about some extraordinary private revelation, but about the witness the Holy Spirit ordinarily gives to all who embrace the grace of the gospel.

If, then, the Holy Spirit assures us that we are children and heirs of God, why should we doubt our predestination? Paul says the same thing in this very letter: “Those whom God predestined he also called; those he called he also justified; and those he justified he also glorified.” What shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who can be against us?

Therefore, if I clearly recognize that God has called me by giving me faith and the fruits of faith—the peace of conscience, the putting to death of the flesh, and the awakening of the spirit, whether fully or in part—why should I doubt that I have been predestined? Moreover, with Paul we say that all true Christians, that is, all who believe the gospel, have received not the spirit of this world but the Spirit who comes from God, through whose inspiration they recognize the gifts God has freely given them. Why should it be surprising, then, that we know he has given them eternal life from before time began?

Some people say that no one should be so arrogant as to boast of possessing the Spirit of Christ. They speak as though Christians claimed to possess him by their own merit instead of through God’s sheer mercy; as though it were arrogant to confess oneself a Christian; and as though someone could be a Christian without possessing Christ’s Spirit. Nor could we say “Jesus is Lord” without hypocrisy, or call God “Father,” unless the Spirit moved our hearts and tongues to speak those sweet words. Yet these very people who accuse us of arrogance because we say God gives the Holy Spirit with faith not only permit us to say the Lord’s Prayer each day but command us to do it.

Let them tell me, then, how faith can possibly be separated from the Holy Spirit, since faith is the Spirit’s own work. If it is arrogance to believe that Christ’s Spirit is in us, why does Paul command the Corinthians to examine themselves to see whether they have faith, declaring them disqualified if they do not recognize that Christ is in them? It is truly a great blindness to accuse Christians of arrogance for daring to rejoice in the presence of the Holy Spirit, when Christianity itself could not stand without this confidence.

Christ cannot lie. He says that his Spirit is unknown to the world and known only to those with whom he dwells. Let these critics, therefore, become true Christians, lay aside their Jewish-minded attitudes toward the Law, sincerely embrace the grace of the gospel, and they will know that Christians possess the Holy Spirit and know that they possess him.

Perhaps someone will object that, without a special revelation, Christians cannot know they enjoy God’s grace and therefore cannot know that they are predestined. Such a person might chiefly appeal to Solomon’s words, “A person does not know whether he is worthy of love or hatred,” or to Paul’s words to the Corinthians, “I am conscious of nothing against myself, yet I am not thereby justified.”

I believe I have already shown clearly from Holy Scripture that this opinion is mistaken. It only remains for me to explain briefly why these two authorities, on which it chiefly rests, should not be understood in that way.

As for Solomon’s words, although the common translation has not rendered them faithfully, any reader who considers Solomon’s whole discussion can see that he means this: anyone who tries to judge whether God loves or hates him by the outward events of the present life is laboring in vain. The same things happen to the righteous and the wicked, to the person who offers sacrifice and the one who does not, to the good person and the sinner. We therefore cannot conclude that God always shows love to those to whom he gives outward prosperity, or hatred to those whom he afflicts.

Do you really think, dearest brother or sister, that we must conclude a person cannot be sure of God’s grace because that assurance cannot be discovered from the changing circumstances of our passing lives? In the same discussion Solomon says that from outward appearances one cannot distinguish the soul of a human being from the life of a beast, for both seem to die in the same way. Are we therefore to conclude, from this outward event, that our conviction of the soul’s immortality rests on nothing but guesswork? There is no need to labor any longer over a point so clear.

As for Paul’s words, I say that he is speaking about his administration of the gospel. He knows of no fault in that ministry, but he does not therefore claim to have fulfilled every duty perfectly or to have earned God’s praise as one who has done all that is fitting for a faithful steward. A just and sensible household steward would not presume to declare himself wholly justified in the performance of his office, as though he had perfectly fulfilled his duty and his master’s will. Rather, he would leave that judgment to his master.

Anyone who reads the words preceding and following Paul’s statement with care will see that this is his meaning. I know that some explain his words as though he were saying that, although he was aware of no sin in himself, he nevertheless could not know himself righteous before God, because, as David says, no one can recognize all his sins perfectly.

But they fail to notice that Paul did not place righteousness in works but in faith; that he rejected every righteousness of his own and embraced only the righteousness God gives through Christ. They also overlook how completely certain Paul was that he had been justified through his steadfast and pure Christian faith. He knew that the crown of righteousness was prepared for him in heaven; he was certain that nothing in heaven, on earth, or beneath the earth could separate him from God’s love; and he longed to die because he knew he would be with Christ. All these things would be false if he had not been certain that he was righteous—righteous through faith, I say, and not through works.

Let us therefore stop making Paul say what he never thought, indeed what he fought against most fiercely when he rebuked those who measured justification by works instead of by faith in Christ our Lord.

Besides these two passages from Solomon and Paul, someone might cite other places in Scripture that exhort people to fear, which might appear opposed to the assurance of predestination. If I explained each of these passages individually, this discussion would grow too long. But I will say in general that fear of punishment belongs to the old covenant, while the love of children belongs to the new. Paul bears witness to this when he says to the Romans, “You did not receive the spirit of slavery again to fear, but the Spirit of adoption, through whom we cry, ‘Abba, Father!’”

To Timothy he says that God has not given us a spirit of fear, but one of power and love. Through Christ, in accordance with the promises made through the mouths of his prophets, God has granted that we, delivered from the hands of our enemies, may serve him without fear, in holiness and righteousness before him all the days of our lives. These and many similar passages of Scripture plainly show that the fear of punishment and servile terror do not belong to the Christian life.

This is further confirmed because such fear is opposed to spiritual joy, which is characteristic of Christians. Paul makes this clear to the Romans when he says that the kingdom of God is righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. That is, everyone who enters the kingdom of gospel grace is justified by faith and consequently enjoys peace of conscience, which brings a holy and complete spiritual joy.

Paul repeatedly urges Christians to rejoice always. Peter likewise says that those who believe in Christ, even while afflicted by various trials, rejoice with an inexpressible and glorious joy.

When Holy Scripture threatens and frightens people who call themselves Christians, we must understand those warnings as addressed to Christians who live carelessly. Because they do not behave as children of God ought, they must be dealt with as servants and held in fear until they taste how good the Lord is, until faith produces its proper effects in them, and until their love as children becomes strong enough to preserve them in godliness and the imitation of Christ.

When the same Scriptures exhort true Christians to fear, they do not mean that believers should fear God’s judgment and wrath as though he were about to condemn them. For, as we have already said, through the Holy Spirit’s testimony to their spirits they know that God has called and chosen them. This is by his pure mercy and not by their merits; therefore they do not doubt that the same mercy will preserve them in the happiness in which he has placed them.

Scripture therefore urges them not to slavish fear but to the reverent fear of children. As good children, we are to guard against dishonoring the Christian life, acting in any way unworthy of God’s children, or grieving the Holy Spirit who dwells within us. Knowing the corruption of our nature, we should remain watchful and alert and never place confidence in ourselves. For desires and passions dwell in our flesh and our hearts; as deadly enemies of the Spirit, they continually lie in wait to make us proud, ambitious, greedy, and sensual.

This is the fear to which Scripture calls true Christians who have already tasted how good the Lord is and who diligently strive to imitate Christ. They put off this fear to the same degree that they put off the old self. Yet good Christians should never wholly cast away this reverence belonging to children, for it is a close friend of Christian love, just as slavish fear is its enemy and cannot exist alongside it.

From all we have said, it is clear that a devout Christian ought not to doubt the forgiveness of sins or the grace of God. Nevertheless, to satisfy the reader more fully, I shall add some testimonies from the holy teachers who confirm this truth.

Hilary, commenting on Matthew in his fifth discourse, says that God wishes us to hope without wavering or uncertainty, because justification by faith is not obtained if faith itself is doubtful. Thus, according to Hilary, a person does not receive forgiveness from God unless he believes without doubt that he will receive it. This is fitting, because anyone who doubts is like a wave of the sea driven and tossed by the wind; such a person should not expect to receive anything from God.

Let us also listen to Augustine. In his manual he teaches us to cast out foolish thoughts that would deprive us of this holy assurance:

“Let foolish agitation murmur as much as it likes, saying: ‘Who are you? How great is that glory? By what merits do you hope to obtain it?’ I answer confidently: ‘I know whom I have believed. I know that through his great love he has made me his child. I know that he is truthful in what he promises, powerful to give what he promises, and able to do whatever he wills.

“‘The multitude of my sins cannot terrify me when I consider the Lord’s death. All my hope rests in his death. His death is my merit, my refuge, my salvation, my life, and my resurrection. My merit is the Lord’s mercy. I am not poor in merit so long as the Lord of mercies does not fail me. And because the Lord’s mercies are many, so great are my merits. The more powerful he is to save, the more secure I am.’”

Elsewhere Augustine, speaking with God, says that he could have despaired because of his great sins and countless failures had the Word not become flesh. Then he adds:

“All my hope and all the assurance of my confidence are set upon his precious blood, which was shed for us and for our salvation. In him I breathe again. Trusting in him, I long to come to you, Father, possessing not my own righteousness but that of your Son Jesus Christ.”

In these passages Augustine plainly shows that Christians should not fear but be assured of their justification, grounding it not in their own works but in Christ’s precious blood, which cleanses us from every sin and makes peace between us and God.

Bernard, in his first sermon on the Annunciation of the Lord, declares most clearly that it is not enough to believe that you can have forgiveness of sins only through God’s kindness; nor is it enough to believe that you can have no good desire or good work unless he grants it; nor is it enough to believe that you cannot deserve eternal life by your works unless it is given freely. Beyond all these things—which, Bernard says, should rather be regarded as a beginning and foundation of faith—you must believe that through him your own sins have been forgiven.

See how this holy man confesses that it is not enough to believe in forgiveness of sins merely as a general truth. You must believe personally that your own wrongdoings have been forgiven through Christ. The reason is plain: since God promises you justification through Christ’s merits, if you do not believe that you are justified by Christ’s merits, you make God out to be a liar and thereby show yourself wholly unworthy of his grace and generosity.

Perhaps you will say, “I certainly believe in forgiveness of sins, and I know God is truthful, but I doubt whether I am worthy of such a gift.” I answer that forgiveness would not be a gift of grace but a payment if God granted it because of the worthiness of your works. I say again that God accepts you as righteous and does not count your sin against you because of Christ’s merits, which are given to you and become yours through faith.

Therefore, following Bernard’s holy counsel, do not believe only in the forgiveness of sins in general. Apply this faith to yourself personally, believing without hesitation that all your wrongdoings are forgiven through Christ. In this way you will glorify God by confessing that he is merciful and truthful; and you will become righteous and holy in his sight, because the righteousness and holiness of Jesus Christ have been shared with you through this faith and confession.

Now, returning to the matter of predestination, I say that from everything discussed above we can plainly understand that assurance of predestination does no harm to true Christians, but brings them the greatest benefit. Nor does it seem to me that it can harm those who are rejected and are false Christians, for even if such people tried hard to persuade themselves that they were numbered among the predestined, they could never convince their own consciences, which would continually protest.

Nevertheless, it may appear that the doctrine of predestination could harm such people, because they are accustomed to saying, “If I am among those rejected, what good will my good works do? And if I am predestined, I shall be saved without troubling myself about good works.”

I reply briefly that such diabolical arguments only increase God’s wrath against them. God revealed the knowledge of predestination to Christians to make them fervent, not cold, in loving him, and ready, not sluggish, in doing good works.

The true Christian, then, on the one hand holds firmly that he has been predestined to eternal life and will be saved—not through his own merits but through God’s election, for God predestined us not because of our works but to display his mercy. On the other hand, this same Christian devotes himself to good works and the imitation of Christ as diligently as if his salvation depended upon his own industry and care.

But anyone who stops doing good because of the doctrine of predestination, saying, “If I am predestined, I shall be saved without the trouble of doing good works,” plainly shows that he was working not from love of God but from self-love. His works may have seemed good and holy in human eyes; but to God, who looks upon the intention, they were wicked and hateful.

We can therefore see that the doctrine of predestination benefits rather than harms false Christians, because it uncovers their hypocrisy. While hypocrisy remains concealed beneath a cloak of outward works, it cannot be healed.

I wish that those who say, “I will not do good, because if I am predestined I shall be saved without effort,” would tell me why they do not reason in the same way when they are ill. Why do they not say, “I want neither physician nor medicine, because whatever God has determined about me must happen”? Why do they eat and drink? Why do they plow fields, plant vineyards, and diligently do everything needed to preserve bodily life? Why do they not say, “All our efforts and work are unnecessary, because whatever God has foreseen and decreed concerning our life and death must come to pass”?

If God’s providence does not make them negligent and idle in matters concerning the body, why should it make them lazy and idle in matters concerning Christian maturity, which is incomparably nobler than the body?

We see that neither Jesus Christ nor Paul refrained from preaching the truth needed to build up the elect merely because those who rejected it might take offense. Indeed, for the sake of the elect the Son of God became man and died upon the cross. Neither, then, should we refrain from proclaiming predestination to true Christians because false Christians may be offended, since we have seen how greatly it builds up believers.

We have now come to the end of our discussion. Our chief purpose throughout has been, according to our small strength, to celebrate and magnify the astonishing benefit Christians have received through Jesus Christ crucified, and to show that faith itself justifies. That is to say, God receives as righteous everyone who truly believes that Jesus Christ has made satisfaction for their sins. Yet just as light cannot be separated from the flame which alone burns, so good works cannot be separated from the faith which alone justifies.

This most holy teaching, which exalts Jesus Christ and humbles human pride, has always been and always will be opposed by Christians whose hearts are set on the Law.

But blessed is the person who, imitating Paul, casts off every claim to righteousness of his own and desires no righteousness except that of Christ. Clothed in that righteousness, such a person may stand with complete confidence before God and receive from him the blessing and inheritance of heaven and earth, together with his only Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, to whom be glory forever. Amen.
