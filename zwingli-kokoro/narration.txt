Huldrych Zwingli. A Remonstrance to Fridolin Lindauer, Concerning His Public Error About Grace Through Christ. Written in Zurich, October twentieth, fifteen twenty-four. Modern English translation for review. Historical note: Zwingli argues that forgiveness rests on the sufficient saving work of Jesus Christ rather than on purchased sacramental absolution. Zwingli defended infant baptism in other writings; this reading is not an endorsement of that doctrine. The following is his complete letter as translated from Latin.

Grace and peace from God the Father and from his only-begotten Son, our Lord Jesus Christ.

Beloved brother in the Lord, if indeed you belong to the Lord: up to this point you have poured out many grave insults, whether through recklessness or boldness, against the pure teaching of the gospel, the people of Zurich, and those of us who preach the Word there. We have held back from answering, chiefly because we hoped that you would change your judgment and, with it, your manner of speaking. Long ago we considered approaching you privately by letter and, if you would not listen, pressing the matter gradually, sometimes exhorting and sometimes correcting you, until you abandoned Egypt, despised its pots of meat and its melons, and refreshed not only your own palate but that of your congregation with the manna of the heavenly Word.

But as your spirit grew fiercer day by day, I changed my plan. I thought it better to allow your anger to spend itself as you raged of your own accord, rather than provoke it further by my writing. You seemed so driven by shamelessness and frenzy that all the medicines of Anticyra would have done you no good. So I resolved to be silent, comforted by the thought that, even if you outdid the frenzied Bacchantes in your excesses, you could do little harm to Christ and even less good to Antichrist.

Now, however, as though it were not enough to quarrel over particular passages of Scripture and to vent your rage on men, you are attempting to overthrow the very sum of the Christian religion, and you are not ashamed to speak blasphemously of Christ

himself. We have therefore decided to break our longstanding silence. We must not appear to consent to an insult against God and so become guilty of betrayal. We must not suffer what happened to the Israelites when they failed to expel the nations God had commanded them to drive out: those peoples remained, provoked God's anger, and repeatedly brought Israel into subjection to enemies whom Israel should have ruled.

Lest the same thing happen to us—lest falsehood and lies bring truth into subjection—your affront to heavenly doctrine and to Christ, the Son of God and of the Virgin, cannot be passed over. So let us come to the issue itself.

Last August, when you preached to the church at the baths of our Swiss countrymen [Baden], you openly asserted two things. First, you said that Christ suffered only for the sins of those who lived before him. Second, you said that through Christ no sins are forgiven to us except those washed away in baptism, because sins committed afterward must be cleansed by the sacraments of the church.

I shall first set before your eyes, briefly but clearly, how terribly you have gone astray in the first assertion. To Abraham the promise was given, in Genesis 15 as you cite it, "In your seed all the families of the earth shall be blessed." If the church called from among the nations—gathered from public squares, streets, and places outside the city walls—is not blessed in Abraham's seed, who is Christ, how will all the families of the earth be blessed in him? Conversely, if all are blessed through Christ, how can you forcibly deprive his descendants of that blessing?

Trace the whole line of Abraham's descendants and you will find a continuous succession of prophecies in words and foreshadowings in figures declaring that Christ would become the Savior of all nations: Israel was to bring forth the Lord, while the nations would embrace him. What, for instance, do you suppose the rejection of Ishmael and Esau signifies, if not the rejection of the former tenants and the letting out of the vineyard anew to the nations? And what could Jacob's return from Mesopotamia signify—with his two wives, with the more beautiful having long been barren, and with splendid offspring and numerous flocks—if not Christ bringing sheep from Jews and Gentiles together into one church, and leading them back to the place from which he himself came?

Isaiah expresses the same thing in a beautiful comparison of a woman who had long been barren and afterward became fruitful. In chapter 54, referring to those who prefigured this event, he says: "Sing, O barren one, who did not bear; break forth into singing and cry aloud, you who have not been in labor! For the children of the desolate one will be more than the children of her who has a husband."

What of the words of the most holy Baptist: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world"? Are these false, or the babbling of some empty talker? Do the Jewish fathers who lived before Christ constitute the world, while we are not part of the world? If Christ has healed our wounds too, why do you begrudge us that salvation?

When certain Gentiles wished to meet Jesus, as John 12 records, he recognized that the time was at hand for the word of salvation

to be proclaimed to the nations too. He said that the hour had come for the Son of Man to be glorified—that is, through his overcoming death, salvation in his name would be promised to all the nations. Do you think that promise was also in vain?

After rising from the dead, he commanded his disciples: "Go, teach all nations, baptizing them," and again, "Preach the gospel to every creature." Do even these commands strike you as empty? Certainly not—unless, as some have the audacity to suggest, the nations are not nations, or the human race has ceased to be part of God's creation. Is God the God of the Jews only? Is he not also the God of the Gentiles? Yes, of the Gentiles too, as Romans 3 says.

But enough of this first point. I hear that you did not persist in it obstinately. Rather, after the fashion of rhetoricians, you demanded an unjust concession so that you could take away what was just—if indeed your remaining claim could ever be just. You confined the promise of salvation through Christ to the ancient fathers, so that those who truly belong to Christ might count themselves fortunate and be satisfied merely to have Christ as a Savior once, and then be led to rely on the signs of the sacraments, which the common run of priests sell at a high price. With their hired mutterings, though cold and half asleep, and their prayers bought for money, Christians are encouraged to try to expiate their offenses.

That was the direction betrayed by your sayings: "It is a good thing to offer," "Offer for the living and for the dead," "Do not listen to those who teach that offerings ought not to be made," and others

like them. Yes, you touched the real matter; that is the cause of your tears! But I return to the subject.

You did not cling very stubbornly to the first opinion, knowing well how impious, Judaizing, and blasphemous its defense would be. You withdrew from it in such a way, however, as to leave yourself an escape through evasion. Then, with the spirit of a gladiator, you hurried to the second assertion, as though determined to defend it not only with axes but with your hands and feet. This was the assertion that through Christ no sins are forgiven to us except those washed away in baptism, and that the sins committed afterward must be expiated by the sacraments.

It is difficult to express how impious this opinion is and how unworthy it is of a Christian, much less of a minister of the Word. What is it to trample Christ underfoot, if this is not? You have so many clear passages of Scripture showing that Christ is the redemption and ransom for all the offenses of all people.

Consider again the forerunner's words: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world." Why does he speak of the sins of the world if Christ removed only original sin? Or is there no sin other than original sin? Must we understand his words about sins to mean nothing but original sin, as though every sin were only original sin? Everyone can see how absurd either conclusion is.

In John 3 you could have read: "God did not send his Son into the world to condemn the world, but that the world might be saved through him. Whoever believes in him is not condemned." There you see that the world is saved through Christ, not merely that

original sin or sins committed before baptism are healed. And the following words, "Whoever believes in him is not condemned," could have led you by the hand to an understanding of faith: through faith in God by Christ all sins are wiped away, so that those who trust in Christ are not even brought under condemnation. This is not the place to discuss the matter at the length it deserves, lest we write a book rather than a remonstrance.

In John 1 it is written: "The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ." If only original sin and the sins preceding baptism are removed through Christ, what difference is there between Moses and Christ? Especially since, according to your own view, circumcision accomplished for the ancients what baptism does for us—the one pointing forward to the Christ who was to come, and the other to Christ already received. What kind of grace would it be to wash someone once, while knowing that he could never again become clean?

Grace, Scripture says, came through Christ so that it might be perpetual, endure forever, and forever cleanse. For "all that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (John 6).

Let us turn to the plain truth. Christ declares in John 14: "I am the way, the truth, and the life." Since he is the way, we must enter into life through him. Those who enter by some other way are thieves and robbers. He says again in the same place: "No one comes to the Father except through me." Thus we do not reach the Father through sacraments or through some other price, but

through Christ. He himself said this, and he is the truth. There is therefore absolutely no road to the Father except Christ.

In that same passage he says: "I go to the Father, and whatever you ask in my name, this I will do." Christ promises that he himself will do what we ask in his name. Therefore, if our iniquities weigh upon us like a heavy burden, we shall pray through Christ to be relieved, and he will do what we ask. He did not say, "You shall be delivered by the power of the sacraments," or "by some other means," but he said, "Whatever you ask in my name, I will do." In chapter 16 he says: "Truly, truly, I say to you, whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give you." Here he promises that the Father will give what he had just promised to give himself, attributing to himself and to the Father alike what comes to us from God.

If, therefore, whatever we ask the Father in Christ's name is given to us, why should we not ask forgiveness for our offenses in his name? Why should we seek it by any road other than Christ?

In Luke 24 you read plainly that Christ opened the minds of the apostles and those who were with them so that they could understand the Scriptures. From those Scriptures he then showed that it was necessary for him to suffer, to rise on the third day, and for repentance and forgiveness of sins to be preached in his name to all nations. Here you have a witness clearer than the sun itself that repentance and forgiveness of sins must be proclaimed in the name of Christ. You cannot explain it away by speaking only of original sin and sins preceding baptism, for the words are general: "repentance and forgiveness of sins." Therefore you cannot say

that Christ is a Savior to the repentant only once. You must confess that repentance and forgiveness of sins are effective in no other name than Christ's, for he commanded that they be proclaimed in his name to all nations.

In Acts 4 Peter declares that there is no other name under heaven by which we must be saved than the name of Jesus. How, then, do you dare assert that our wounds are wiped away by sacraments, when they are healed by the blood of Christ alone? It is the blood of the covenant, shed for the salvation of the whole multitude.

But why do I pursue this question at such length? The new covenant was established so that by the everlasting blood of the eternal Son of God we might have perpetual access to God. We are not to be compelled, like the Jews under the old rites, to be stained rather than cleansed by the blood of cattle and goats. Instead, whenever we receive a wound, we should cry to the Father that he would heal it—that is, forgive it—at the price paid by his Son. The one who commanded us to ask in his name is truthful; therefore we shall obtain what we seek. No one can deny this: the Son who showed us this way is one with the Father, and what he promises to do himself he elsewhere promises that the Father will do.

Why should I now produce the testimony of the apostles? Their entire teaching establishes Christ as the Savior of all nations, from every disease of the soul and every wound of conscience.

But you will say: "I do not deny anything that has been brought forward. Christ is indeed the Lamb who takes away the sins of the world, but he takes them away only once for each person." Who has ever said that? "Only once"! The fact that Christ died only once

does not imply that he reconciles us only once. Because he is the Son of the eternal God, the price of his blood endures forever to take away every sin of all people—not once only, but always. He lives forever for this purpose, and always sits at the Father's right hand, so that he continually appeases the just wrath of God.

Nor is this an invention of ours. In 1 John 2 you have: "My little children, I write these things to you so that you may not sin. But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous. He is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world."

First, to whom is John writing? To believers, of course. Therefore he promises Christ as an advocate and a propitiation to believers who had undoubtedly already been baptized. Second, in saying "if anyone does sin," John is plainly not speaking of the original disease: we did not ourselves commit Adam's first sin; Adam did. Finally, you hear again that Christ is the sacrifice that expiates not only the sins of one nation but all the offenses of the whole world.

Christ himself taught the same thing in that most delightful parable of the Samaritan, when he undertook to make good even what was spent beyond the two denarii. And what does Paul do throughout the Epistle to the Hebrews but explain the power and character of Christ's death? He shows that Christ, by one death and offering, has perfected for all time those who are sanctified—that is, the faithful. He contrasts the sacrifices of old, which had to be repeated frequently, with Christ's offering, so holy, so rich, and so completely sufficient that all who trust in it may continually approach God through it.

Thus Hebrews 13 says: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever." It surely means that Christ remains the Savior until the consummation of the world, just as he was when he gave up his spirit on the cross. The writer soon adds: "Do not be led away by diverse and strange teachings, for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace." He thus makes human salvation depend on the grace of God alone through Christ, who opened this new and living way, as Hebrews 10 teaches.

It is by God's grace alone, therefore, that we are restored to blessedness. And God's grace has been established through Christ so firmly that it has almost taken on the character of a debt—not that God owes us anything by our merit, but because of the certainty of his gracious promise. For how shall he not give us all things, when he did not spare his own Son?

Here is the final point, and the one most opposed to your assertion: we are justified by God's grace alone, not by the sacraments that you would gladly sell at a high price. Paul explains this most clearly in his letters both to the Romans and to the Galatians. If sins could have been abolished by sacraments, why did Christ need to put on flesh? Why did he not simply institute rites by which we might be cleansed?

You will reply that the sacraments cleanse sins not by their own power but through the power of Christ's suffering. Have you forgotten even Aristotle's maxim: that because of which a thing has a quality possesses that quality more fully? If the sacraments cleanse because they have received their efficacy from Christ's

blood, then it is the blood, not the sacraments, that washes away offenses.

Those, therefore, who trust without fear in the blood of Christ obtain forgiveness of sins. When sacraments are added, they instruct the outward person rather than transform the inward person. The inward person cannot be taught or made faithful by physical elements, just as the outward person cannot apprehend what he does not experience through the senses.

Therefore, to address the whole person—both inward and outward—God commanded, for example, that a person who had already believed should be dipped in water. He did not intend by this means to wash the soul. How could an incorporeal substance be washed by a corporeal element? Rather, the outward person was to be initiated by a visible sign, so that he might become more certain of what takes place within by the light of faith or by the manifest Word of God's grace.

Augustine saw this rightly when he said that even when the Word is joined to the element, faith nevertheless accomplishes the whole matter. But let us count the testimony of Augustine, as well as our own, as of little weight unless the Word of God plainly teaches the same thing.

In 1 Peter 3 it is written: "Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you—not the removal of dirt from the flesh, but the appeal of a good conscience toward God, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."

First, you see that baptism here is taken for faith, because faith alone saves us. It is neither strange nor unusual for the apostles to use the sign for the thing signified; Paul does so frequently, and most plainly in the Epistle to the Romans. Second, Peter himself immediately makes the meaning plain with the words that follow: "not the removal of dirt from the flesh." From this it is evident that baptism does not save insofar as it washes the body's surface and its dirt, but insofar as our conscience answers rightly and honestly before God. Examining itself, the conscience recognizes its standing before God and is certain of life in Christ, because Christ has risen from the dead.

Why say more? Let this be the principal point: WE ARE SAVED BY THE GRACE OF GOD ALONE, AND THIS WHEN WE TRUST IN THAT GRACE.

Away, then, with your opinion, so insulting to God's grace, by which you assert that sins afterward committed must be erased by the sacraments and by no other means. Since you have publicly taught this to the injury of the Son of God, I beg you, by that Son himself, to repent, recall your mind from error, and yield it to obedience to God. Do not permit any person's word to possess as much authority with you as God's Word.

If you do so, you will become a new man. You will condemn what you once obstinately maintained from the dreams of men. The scales will fall from your eyes, and then you will look more closely and steadily upon the truth. If you truly contemplate its face, you will be ashamed of your former presumption, by which you

affirmed so many errors—not only this one—so loudly and confidently, without the authority of God's Word.

Your spirit will also become more humane and moderate, so that you will not imitate those who stir up fresh fires every day. At one moment they shout that people who rely upon God's Word are heretics; at another they cry out that those same people should be put to death without even having their case heard. The Lord will one day scatter their rage and uproar.

Instead, when you hear anything that disagrees with your opinion, restrain your judgment for a time and consult the Word of God. Seek it not in stagnant pools but in its very fountain and spring. At the same time, pray that he who freely invited you to drink would grant you to draw the heavenly water—that is, pure knowledge. I do not doubt that you will then judge differently from the way you did when you took counsel with flesh and blood, for all flesh is grass.

But if you stubbornly persist in your insulting error, trampling on God's grace and asserting the presumptuous opinions of flesh and blood, we too shall persevere in refuting you—not only you, but also certain other ignorant little teachers whom we have indulged for far too long, solely because of their arrogant folly.

As for you, whom I know only by reputation, though you live nearby: because people deny that you are swollen by such a great degree of pride, I thought it best to approach you first in friendship. My hope is that, by God's grace, I may gain a brother, so that henceforth you may hold faithfully to God's Word, cast off the snare of men, and, having contemplated the true glory of God,

apply to the miserable consciences of mortals no medicine except what has been drawn from the pharmacy of the heavenly Physician.

May the great and good God grant this. Amen.

Take in good part this friendly remonstrance of ours, sent later than we intended because of other duties. Farewell.

From Zurich, the twentieth day of October, in the year 1524.

Huldrych Zwingli, yours sincerely, if you would rather belong to Christ than to men.

End of the letter, written in Zurich on October twentieth, fifteen twenty-four.
