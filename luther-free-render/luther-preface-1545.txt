Martin Luther — Preface to His Latin Writings (1545)
Complete fresh English translation

Martin Luther sends greetings to the devout reader.

For a long time I strongly resisted those who wanted my books published—or, more accurately, the confused products of my late-night labors. I did so partly because I did not want the writings of the ancients to be overshadowed by my novelties and readers thereby kept from reading them. Also, by God's grace, many well-ordered books now exist. Among them Philip's Commonplaces stands out; through such books a theologian and bishop may be thoroughly trained to speak the teaching of godliness with power. Above all, the Holy Bible can now be obtained in almost every language. But my books, having been produced according to the disorder of events as they happened, are themselves a rough, unorganized chaos that even I would now find difficult to arrange.

For these reasons I wanted all my books buried in perpetual forgetfulness, to make room for better ones. But the tiresome persistence of others prevailed. Day after day they filled my ears with the warning that, if I did not permit publication while I lived, then after my death people ignorant of the causes and circumstances of these events would surely publish them. Thus one confusion would give rise to many. Their insistence finally won, and I permitted publication. There was also the wish and command of our most illustrious prince, Elector John Frederick, who ordered—indeed compelled—the printers not only to print the books but to hurry the edition along.

Above all, I ask the devout reader, for the sake of our Lord Jesus Christ himself, to read these writings with judgment and, more than that, with great compassion. Let him remember that I was once a monk and a most fervent adherent of the pope when I first entered this controversy. I was so intoxicated, indeed so submerged, in papal teaching that I was ready, if I could, to kill or assist and approve of killing anyone who refused obedience to the pope by even a single syllable. Such a Saul was I, as many still are. I was not cold as ice in defending the papacy, as Eck and those like him were; they seemed to me to defend the pope for the sake of their stomachs rather than from earnest conviction. Even today they seem to me to laugh at the pope like Epicureans. I, on the contrary, acted earnestly. I feared the last day terribly, and from the depths of my being I longed to be saved.

Therefore in my earliest writings you will find how many great things I humbly conceded to the pope—things I now regard and condemn as the utmost blasphemy and abomination. Attribute this error, devout reader, or the contradiction as my opponents call it, to the time and to my ignorance. At first I stood alone, wholly unfit and unlearned for handling such great matters. I fell into these disputes by chance, not by choice or deliberate purpose; God himself is my witness.

And so, when indulgences were being sold in these regions in 1517—'proclaimed' is what I meant to say—for the sake of the most shameful profit, I was then a preacher, a young doctor of theology as they say. I began warning the people not to listen to the shouting of the indulgence sellers, for they had better things to do. I was confident that in this I had the pope as my protector. I then relied firmly upon him, since in his decrees he plainly condemned the immoderation of the profiteers, as he called those who preached indulgences.

Soon I wrote two letters: one to Archbishop Albert of Mainz, who received half the money from the indulgences, while the pope received the other half—something I did not then know—and the other to the local ordinary, Bishop Jerome of Brandenburg. I asked them to restrain the impudence and blasphemy of the profiteers. But I was despised as a poor little friar. When I was disregarded, I published a short set of theses for disputation, together with a German sermon on indulgences, and a little later my Explanations. In these I sought, for the pope's honor, not to condemn indulgences outright but to place good works of love above them.

That was treated as though I had cast heaven down and set the whole world on fire. I was accused before the pope; a summons to Rome was sent to me; the entire papacy rose against me alone. This happened in 1518, during the imperial diet held by Maximilian at Augsburg. Cardinal Cajetan was there as the pope's legate. My gracious lord Frederick, elector of Saxony, approached him on my behalf and secured the agreement that I should not be forced to go to Rome, but that Cajetan should summon me, examine the matter, and settle it. Soon afterward the diet ended.

Meanwhile the Germans, exhausted by the plundering, trafficking, and endless frauds of the Roman scoundrels, awaited the outcome with anxious minds. No bishop or theologian before had dared touch such a matter. Public feeling supported me somewhat, because everybody already hated the tricks and Roman dealings with which they had filled and wearied the world.

So I came to Augsburg on foot and in poverty, supplied with funds and letters of recommendation from Prince Frederick to the city council and certain honorable men. I was there for three days before I approached the cardinal, because those excellent men urgently warned me not to go without a safe-conduct from the emperor. Yet the cardinal summoned me daily by means of a messenger. The messenger was very troublesome, saying that I needed only to recant and everything would be well. But the wrong done to me was lengthy, and the proceedings were tangled.

At last, on the third day, the messenger came demanding to know why I would not approach the cardinal, who, he said, was waiting for me with great kindness. I answered that I must obey the advice of the excellent men to whom Prince Frederick had commended me. They advised me not to appear before the cardinal without imperial protection or a public safe-conduct; once it had been obtained, as they were seeking it from the imperial council, I would come immediately. He grew agitated. 'What!' he said. 'Do you imagine Prince Frederick will take up arms for your sake?' 'I would certainly not want that,' I answered. 'And where will you stay?' 'Under the sky,' I said. 'If the pope and cardinals were in your power, what would you do with them?' 'Show them every reverence and honor,' I replied. Then, wagging his finger in an Italian gesture, he exclaimed, 'Ha!' and went away, never to return.

That day the imperial council informed the cardinal that I had been granted the emperor's protection and safe-conduct, and warned him not to act harshly against me. He is reported to have said, 'Very well; nevertheless I shall do my duty.' These were the beginnings of that disturbance. The rest may be learned from the records that follow.

In the same year Master Philip Melanchthon had been called here by Prince Frederick to teach Greek, doubtless so that I might have a companion in the work of theology. What the Lord accomplished through this instrument, not only in languages but in theology, his writings testify sufficiently, however angry Satan and his whole brood may be.

The following year, 1519, Maximilian died in February, and Duke Frederick became imperial vicar according to the law of the empire. The storm then abated for a time, and contempt for excommunication and the papal thunderbolt gradually crept in. Eck and Caracciolo brought from Rome the bull condemning Luther and delivered their complaints to Frederick, who was then at Cologne to receive the newly elected Charles together with the other princes. Frederick was deeply offended and sharply rebuked the papal agent for stirring up his territories and those of his brother John in his absence. He treated them so firmly that they departed in shame and disgrace. The prince, endowed with extraordinary insight, understood the devices of the Roman court and knew how to deal with them. He had an exceedingly keen nose and could smell farther ahead than the Romans could either hope or fear.

From then on they refrained from testing him. When Leo the Tenth sent him the so-called Golden Rose that same year, Frederick did not consider it worthy of any honor but rather treated it as a joke. The Roman party was forced to despair of deceiving such a prince. Under the protection of this prince the gospel advanced happily and spread widely. His authority moved many, since such a wise and clear-sighted prince could hardly be suspected of wishing to nourish or defend heresy except by the envious. This dealt the papacy a great blow.

In the same year the Leipzig disputation took place. Eck challenged both Karlstadt and me. I could not, however, obtain a safe-conduct from Duke George by any letter, so I entered Leipzig not as a disputant but as a spectator under the protection granted to Karlstadt. I do not know who hindered me, for I was certain that Duke George was not yet hostile to me.

Eck approached me in my lodging and said that he had heard I was refusing to debate. I replied, 'How can I debate when I cannot obtain a safe-conduct from Duke George?' 'If I cannot debate you,' he answered, 'I do not want to debate Karlstadt either, for I came here because of you. What if I obtain a safe-conduct for you? Will you debate me?' 'Obtain it,' I said, 'and it shall be done.' He went away, and soon I too was given safe-conduct and permission to debate.

Eck did this because he saw certain glory awaiting him in my assertion that the pope is not head of the church by divine right. Here was a wide field and a splendid occasion to flatter the pope before an audience, win his favor, and bury me under hatred and envy. He worked hard at this throughout the debate; yet he neither established his own claims nor refuted mine. Indeed, over a meal Duke George himself said to Eck and me, 'Whether by human right or divine right, he is still pope.' He would never have said this, but would merely have agreed with Eck, if the arguments had not moved him.

Here you may see in my own case how difficult it is to struggle free and rise out of errors confirmed by the example of the entire world and changed into something like nature by long habit. How true is the proverb, 'It is hard to abandon accustomed ways,' and 'Habit is a second nature.' How rightly Augustine says, 'Habit, if not resisted, becomes necessity.' I had by then spent seven years reading and teaching the sacred writings with utmost diligence, privately and publicly, until I held almost all of them in my memory. I had also tasted the firstfruits of the knowledge and faith of Christ: that we are made righteous and saved not by works but by faith in Christ. Moreover, I was already publicly defending the point just mentioned, that the pope is not head of the church by divine right. Yet I did not see what followed from this: that the pope must necessarily be from the devil. For what is not from God must be from the devil.

As I said, I was so absorbed both by the example and the title of the holy church and by my own habitual thinking that I conceded the pope a human right. Yet such a right, unless it rests on divine authority, is false and devilish. For we obey parents and magistrates not simply because they command, but because this is God's will, as First Peter teaches. For this reason I can bear somewhat more patiently those who stubbornly cling to the papacy, especially those who have read neither sacred nor secular writings, since I myself, even after many years of most diligent reading of Scripture, remained so tightly bound.

In 1519 Leo the Tenth sent the Rose, as I have said, through Charles von Miltitz, who held many conversations with me in order to reconcile me to the pope. He had seventy apostolic briefs so that, if Prince Frederick handed me over to him as the pope sought by means of the Rose, he could post one in each town and convey me safely to Rome. But he revealed the thoughts of his heart to me. 'O Martin,' he said, 'I thought you were some elderly theologian who sat behind his stove disputing with himself. Now I see that you are still young and strong. If I had twenty-five thousand armed men, I would not trust myself to bring you to Rome. Along the whole way I have sounded out what people think of you. For every one I found standing with the pope, I found three standing with you against the pope.'

One episode was particularly amusing. He had even asked women and girls in the inns what they thought of the Roman See. Not understanding that expression, and thinking of an ordinary seat, they replied, 'How could we know what sort of seats you have in Rome—wooden ones or stone ones?'

So he asked me to consider measures that would make for peace and promised to do all he could to bring the pope to do likewise. I too promised at length that I would most readily do anything I could with a good conscience and without compromising the truth. I was myself eager for peace. I had been dragged into these troubles by force, and necessity had compelled everything I had done. The blame was not mine.

Miltitz also summoned John Tetzel, of the Dominican order, the chief author of this tragedy. With papal words and threats he so crushed that man, formerly a terrifying and fearless shouter, that Tetzel wasted away and finally died from distress of mind. When I heard of his condition, I wrote kindly to console him before his death. I told him to take courage and not to fear my memory. But perhaps it was his conscience and the pope's indignation that brought him down.

Charles and his plan were considered foolish. But in my judgment, if the archbishop of Mainz had adopted this plan from the outset, when I warned him, or if the pope had adopted it before condemning me unheard and raging with his bulls, and if they had immediately curbed Tetzel's madness, the affair would not have grown into such an upheaval. The entire fault belongs to the archbishop of Mainz. His wisdom and cunning failed him when he tried both to suppress my teaching and to keep safe the money obtained through indulgences. Now counsels are sought in vain and efforts assembled in vain. The Lord has awakened and stands to judge the peoples. Even if they could kill us, they would not obtain what they want; indeed, they would obtain less than they do while we remain alive and unharmed. Some among them, whose noses are not wholly blunt, understand this well enough.

Meanwhile, in that same year, I had returned to explaining the Psalter for a second time. I was more confident because I had become more practiced after lecturing on Saint Paul's letters to the Romans and Galatians and the letter to the Hebrews. I had been seized with an extraordinary eagerness to understand Paul in his letter to the Romans. Until then one expression, not any coldness of heart, had stood in the way: in the first chapter, 'The righteousness of God is revealed in it.' I hated the expression 'righteousness of

God,' because by the ordinary teaching of all the doctors I had learned to understand it philosophically as the formal or active righteousness, as they call it, by which God is righteous and punishes sinners and the unrighteous.

As for me, though I lived as a monk without outward reproach, I felt myself a sinner before God, with a conscience that could find no rest. I could not trust that my satisfactions had appeased him. I did not love the righteous God who punishes sinners; I hated him. Secretly, if not with outright blasphemy, then certainly with immense complaint, I was indignant against God. I said, 'As though it were not enough for wretched sinners, eternally lost through original sin, to be weighed down by every kind of calamity through the law of the Ten Commandments, must God add sorrow to sorrow through the gospel, and through the gospel also threaten us with his righteousness and wrath?' Thus I raged with a fierce and troubled conscience. Yet I persistently hammered at Paul in that passage, thirsting most intensely to know what Saint Paul meant.

At last, by God's mercy, I meditated day and night upon the connection of the words: 'The righteousness of God is revealed in it, as it is written: The righteous person shall live by faith.' Then I began to understand the righteousness of God as that by which the righteous person lives through God's gift, namely, through faith. I understood that the meaning is this: through the gospel the righteousness of God is revealed—a passive righteousness, by which the merciful God justifies us through faith, as it is written, 'The righteous person shall live by faith.' At this I felt that I had been born completely anew and had entered paradise itself through open gates. Immediately the whole of Scripture appeared to me with a different face.

Then I ran through the Scriptures as my memory supplied them and gathered corresponding expressions elsewhere: the work of God, meaning the work God performs in us; the power of God, by which he makes us powerful; the wisdom of God, by which he makes us wise; the strength of God, the salvation of God, the glory of God. Now I exalted the sweetest expression 'righteousness of God' with a love as great as the hatred with which I had formerly hated it. Thus that passage of Paul became for me truly the gate of paradise.

Afterward I read Augustine's On the Spirit and the Letter and, unexpectedly, discovered that he too understood the righteousness of God in a similar way: the righteousness with which God clothes us when he justifies us. Although he expressed the matter imperfectly and did not explain everything clearly about imputation, it pleased me nevertheless that he taught a righteousness of God by which we are justified.

Armed with these thoughts, I began explaining the Psalter for the second time. The work would have grown into a large commentary if I had not been summoned in the following year to Worms for the imperial diet of Emperor Charles the Fifth and forced to abandon what I had begun.

I tell you these things, excellent reader, so that if you read my little writings you will remember that I was, as I said above, one of those who, as Augustine writes of himself, have made progress by writing and teaching. I am not one of those who suddenly become supreme out of nothing, though they are nothing, having neither labored, nor been tested, nor gained experience, but who imagine that at a single glance at Scripture they have exhausted its whole spirit.

Thus far the indulgence affair had advanced into the years 1520 and 1521. Afterward came the controversies over the sacraments and the Anabaptists, concerning which I shall write prefaces in other

volumes, if I live.

Farewell in the Lord, reader, and pray that the Word may increase against Satan, for he is powerful and wicked, and now especially furious and savage, knowing that his time is short and the kingdom of his pope is in danger. May God strengthen in us what he has worked and bring to completion the work he has begun in us, for his own glory. Amen.

5 March 1545.
