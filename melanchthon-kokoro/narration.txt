Editor’s Introduction: Grace Through Faith in Christ Alone. In the sixteenth century, the Protestant Reformation proclaimed that sinful human beings are justified before a holy God not by their own righteousness, religious efforts, or good works, but through faith in Jesus Christ.
 Philipp Melanchthon, Martin Luther's close collaborator and a principal theologian of the German Reformation, devoted much of his life to explaining this doctrine. In 1552 he responded to Andreas Osiander, whose understanding of justification sparked intense controversy. At the heart of the debate was a question of profound importance: on what basis can a sinner stand righteous before God?
 The gospel points us to Jesus Christ as the only Savior. All have sinned and fall short of God's glory. We cannot earn forgiveness through obedience, moral improvement, church membership, baptism, communion, or any other religious performance.
 Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God, became truly man, lived without sin, died for sinners, and rose again. His sacrifice is sufficient to reconcile sinners to God. Salvation is God's free gift, received through faith in Christ alone. Saving faith is not simply acknowledging Christian propositions or performing a religious act; it is trusting the Lord Jesus Christ and His saving promises.
 Good works and spiritual renewal matter, but they are the fruits of salvation, not the foundation upon which sinners are accepted by God. The distinction between justification and sanctification remains essential. God genuinely transforms those whom He saves, yet confidence before Him rests on Jesus Christ, not on the completeness of that transformation.
 Ephesians 2:8–9 (KJV): 'For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast.'
 Romans 5:1 (KJV): 'Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.'
 This historical work invites readers and listeners to examine the biblical foundation of justification and understand why the Reformers considered it vital for the comfort of troubled consciences. Our purpose is not to exalt any theologian or denomination, but to direct attention to Jesus Christ, the only Savior of sinners.
 Soli Deo Gloria.
 

Theological and Historical Disclaimer.

 This edition presents Melanchthon's own response to Andreas Osiander in a modern English working translation checked against the 1552 printed source. The additional
 attestations by Bugenhagen and Forster, and the appended 1536 disputation, are separate historical texts and are not narrated here. Publishing his work will not imply endorsement of all his theological positions. Melanchthon's later formulations concerning human freedom, conversion, and good works became controversial. His views on baptism and the Lord's Supper should likewise be evaluated by Scripture.
 We affirm that salvation is by grace alone, through faith alone, in Jesus Christ alone; that Jesus Christ is the one mediator between God and humanity; that baptism and the Lord's Supper do not automatically confer justification by the performance of the rite; and that no church, priest, saint, sacrament, human work, or merit can add to Christ's finished sacrifice. Good works necessarily follow living faith but neither earn nor complete justification. Holy Scripture, not any Reformer, is the final authority.
 The historical 1552 printed pamphlet contains additional Latin materials by Johannes Bugenhagen and Johannes Forster and a report concerning Melanchthon and Luther. These must be attributed independently rather than represented as Melanchthon's own words.

Philipp Melanchthon. Answer to Andreas Osiander on Justification, fifteen fifty-two.

 To the Christian Reader.

 Philipp Melanchthon wishes God's grace, through Jesus Christ, our only Mediator and Savior, to the Christian reader.
 I was very glad to hear that many true churches—in which, by God's grace, there are undoubtedly many preachers and other persons who are genuine members of Christ—have asked that their judgment concerning Osiander's book be made known. Since he has singled me out by name and attacked me harshly, I welcome the judgment of other understanding Christian men, as though they were judges. I have always submitted myself and all my writings to the true churches in these lands and cities where the pure doctrine of the gospel is preached, and I remain willing to do so. For this reason I have remained silent until now, praying to God, and I still pray every day, that for the sake of His honor He would explain and preserve His truth, so that we may call upon and praise Him rightly, and that He may graciously continue to gather an everlasting church among us.
 But since I have now been instructed to speak on the matter, and have been urged to do so by many distinguished and other persons, I have set down this clear and simple judgment, neither at great length nor in a contentious spirit. I intend it as a guide for the many people who, because of this dispute about so important a matter, have fallen into deep distress and doubt, so that they may understand what the dispute concerns and what consolation their souls need.
 And I say once again that I have never intended, in my writings, and especially concerning this most important article, to teach anything contrary to the opinion of the venerable Doctor Martin Luther and to the common understanding of the discerning men in these churches. I also have no doubt that this doctrine is the one true teaching revealed through the Son of God and set forth in the writings of the prophets and apostles.
 As for Osiander's reproaches against me in excessively harsh words, he does me an injustice. I will leave this to God, who sees the hearts of all people and is their Judge.
 Everyone knows how I have lived and taught, and I am amazed at the great bitterness that has arisen against me. I was drawn into these great matters against my own wishes, and I acknowledge that I am altogether too insignificant for them. During a time when many confused opinions were circulating, I therefore endeavored to gather together the substance of true and necessary doctrine, and, as far as I could, to speak about it clearly. In this I sincerely intended to serve poor young people. I know very well that all my writings are far too inadequate and weak; for that reason I have also always submitted them to the judgment of our churches. Much more could be said
 about this, but I shall leave it aside for now and, in God's name, speak about the controversy.
 Saint Paul says in Romans 5 that God's grace, and the gift through the kindness of the one man Jesus Christ, have abounded to many more powerfully than sin. Here Paul distinguishes two things. There is grace: the gracious forgiveness of sins and our acceptance as persons before God. And at the same time there is given the gift: God's presence within us, through which we are renewed, with the sure comfort and beginning of eternal life.
 We have both of these through the merit of Jesus Christ. The passage plainly says that these things come to us because of the love that the eternal Father has for His Son, whom Paul here calls the man Jesus Christ. We do not earn them by our works; rather, we obtain them only through faith in the Lord Jesus Christ. This faith is kindled in us in genuine conversion when we hear the gospel, through which the Lord Christ Himself works. For God is made known through His eternal Word and the Holy Spirit.
 This faith must continually receive and hold fast both grace and the gift, on account of Christ the Mediator, even after the new birth has begun.
 John speaks in the same way: grace and truth came through the Lord Jesus Christ. Grace certainly means the gracious forgiveness of sins and the gracious acceptance of our persons, without our merit, for the sake of Jesus Christ. But truth means, in what follows, God's presence within us, eternal life, eternal holiness, blessedness, and joy. Thus he means that the gospel does not bring mere shadows or perishable gifts but true and imperishable blessings. These must be received by faith, as has already been said. This faith trusts continually in the whole Lord Christ, both God and man, just as Christ Himself, both God and man, is Mediator and Redeemer according to both natures. Although it was His human nature alone that experienced wounds and suffering, the whole Christ is Mediator and Redeemer. For those sufferings would not have been the payment for us if the Redeemer were not also God. Moreover, this Mediator must restore life and blessedness and crush the serpent's head; therefore He is also God.
 We accordingly confess plainly, and have always taught, as all the churches can testify, that renewal must truly take place within us. God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit certainly work comfort and life within us in conversion, and are present and dwell in us when the gospel is received by faith. Through this the eternal Word, the Son of God, works and gathers to Himself a church. Thus, when the Son of God proclaimed the gospel in Paradise and spoke the promise that the woman's seed would crush the serpent's head, He at the same time wrought comfort and life in the hearts of Adam and Eve, making the Father known to them.
 As John says, the Son who is in the Father's bosom has made Him known; and again, in Him was life, and the life was the light of men. Thus this Son, Jesus Christ, continually gathers His everlasting church, preserves the preaching ministry and works through
 it, and grants the Holy Spirit, who brings comfort, prayer, and strength. As it is written in First John: by this we know that we abide in Him and He in us, because He has given us of His Spirit. Again, the Psalm says that the sun has its dwelling among them. Christ is the sun in His church, and gives light and life to all who seek refuge in Him with faith through genuine conversion. Again, Ephesians 3 says that Christ dwells in our hearts through faith; and Ephesians 2 speaks of the true Emmanuel, that is, God with us.
 This divine presence is the matter of which Osiander speaks; concerning its reality there is no disagreement between us.
 Osiander nevertheless burdens our churches as though they said nothing about God's presence within us. In doing so he plainly wrongs us. Although it is true that God dwells in those who have been converted, as John 14 says—"We will come to him and make our home with him"—and although the everlasting life that the Lord Christ works, according to His promise, "I give them eternal life," must begin during this present life, there is nevertheless a difference between the saints after the resurrection and the saints in this present life. Paul speaks of this in Galatians 5: through the Spirit, by faith, we await the hope of righteousness. The righteousness for which we still hope and wait is not the forgiveness of sins; it is that God will be all in all, and, as First John 3 says, that we shall be like Him.
 But in this present life, although God dwells in the saints, our old nature is still full of great impurity and sinful defects and inclinations. As the Psalm says, no living person is righteous before You. Therefore the saints, too, must have consolation and must know how they obtain forgiveness of sins and grace—that is, how they are pleasing to God.
 This consolation is set before them: even after regeneration they continually receive and retain forgiveness of sins and grace for the sake of the Mediator, Jesus Christ, through the merit of His obedience, by which He became an offering for us. Concerning this obedience, Psalm 40 says: "In the book it is written of Me; I delight to do Your will, O my God."
 To this belongs the principal saying of Romans 3, which applies to all people at conversion and afterward: we are justified without our own merit, freely by His grace, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God has set forth as the propitiation through faith in His blood.
 It is plain that this text speaks of Christ's obedience and merit in His human nature, just as the word for redemption or ransom indicates the payment and deliverance made known through Him.
 And the words "we are justified" cannot mean merely that we become righteous through the essential righteousness of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We must also grasp forgiveness of sins and grace and distinguish the merit of the Son—who was
 appointed Redeemer—from the Father and the Holy Spirit.
 Some dispute even the expression "through the merit of Christ" and say that it is not found in Scripture. I answer that Scripture often uses a still stronger term for ransom or redemption, a word that properly signifies a payment made for someone's deliverance. To understand Christ's honor we must recognize both His merit and His work. He saves by His merit and by imparting Himself.
 The following passages speak plainly about His merit. Romans 5: we have been justified by His blood; again, through the obedience of one man many will be made righteous. Osiander interprets these and similar passages very differently. He says that the obedience of the one means the divinity that made Him obedient, by which others are made obedient as well. Yet the passage manifestly speaks of merit. Every discerning reader can judge that if this unusual interpretation were right, Paul would have said nothing of Christ's humanity, even though he expressly says, "through the obedience of one man."
 First John 1: the blood of Jesus Christ cleanses us from all sin. Hebrews 10: we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all. Isaiah 53: by His wounds we are healed.
 All these statements must be understood to mean that we have forgiveness of sins and are accepted before God through Christ's merit when, with true faith, we receive the Lord Christ and believe that for the sake of this Mediator God desires to forgive our sins and show us grace. It is also true at the same time that God dwells within us, as we are raised up through this consolation.
 This doctrine and consolation—that even after regeneration the saints lament their former sins and inborn uncleanness and seek comfort in the Mediator through faith—has been preached continually from the beginning by the prophets, in every saying in which they teach us to seek refuge in God's mercy. For that mercy is promised for the sake of the Mediator and on account of His merit. Thus the prophets trusted in the Redeemer, who is God and the promised seed of Abraham. Daniel says, "Hear us not because of our righteousness, but for the sake of Your great mercy, for the Lord's sake." Again, blessed are those whose sins are covered.
 Osiander often says, "By righteousness I mean that which causes us to act rightly within ourselves." In these words nothing is said about the forgiveness of sins. We, by contrast, call Christ the Lord our righteousness, through whom we have forgiveness of sins and a gracious God, and through whom we also have God's presence within us. Paul gathers all this together in his chief saying in Romans 3: we are justified freely by grace through Christ, whom God appointed as the propitiation to be received through faith in His blood. And Isaiah 53 says, "My righteous servant will make many righteous through the knowledge of him." In this Paul and Isaiah undoubtedly include both grace and the gift, as explained above.
 Let everyone consider the great seriousness and anguish of conscience. Suppose a person is dying and feels the wrath of God. Such a person must be directed to Christ, who remains, continually and even after regeneration, our Mediator, High Priest, and Intercessor, and who is the shelter under which we find grace and consolation. The Lord Himself teaches us this when He says, "Come to Me, all you who are troubled."
 All the prophets likewise teach us to pray in this way. David cries, "O Lord, do not rebuke me in Your anger; save me for Your mercy's sake." Although God also dwells within us, the joy of God's presence does not shine equally brightly at every moment. When David was pursued by the terror of God's anger over his former sins and present sorrows, and when Absalom stirred up rebellion and dishonored his household, he needed comfort in the promised Savior. That is where the heart must turn.
 Thus all of us pray daily, calling upon our Mediator Jesus Christ, God and man, casting ourselves upon His wounds, and seeking this true comfort: we have forgiveness of sins and are heard for the sake of the Mediator. Though God dwells and works within us amid this consolation, as Scripture also says, "The Spirit intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words," God nevertheless directs our faith to the Lord Christ, God and man, and to His merit and intercession. Christ's own saying is, "Whatever you ask the Father in My name, He will give you." Thus the Lord Christ Himself and the prophets teach us to pray.
 It follows that whoever would open people's eyes to this comfort and turn them instead to an essential righteousness within themselves should consider the matter carefully. The words "righteousness" and "the Holy Spirit who makes us righteous" are indeed closely related, but they are not simply identical; there is a relation of cause and effect.
 Osiander objects that the teaching, "We are righteous—that is, pleasing to God—solely for the sake of the Lord Christ through faith," will make people careless and secure in sin. The answer is that we must teach rightly, give God His honor, and condemn sin. At the same time, genuine comfort must be set before frightened consciences. Not all hearers are alike, and Christ's hearers were not all alike either.
 The beautiful saying in Jeremiah 23 speaks of the whole Lord Christ and the fullness of His benefits: "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch," and, "This is the name by which He will be called: The Lord our righteousness." The prophet speaks of one Person descended from David's line who is at the same time true God. Of this Person he says that He is our righteousness. We must not exclude from that expression either forgiveness of sins or Christ's merit, but understand righteousness to mean that through Him we have both grace and the gift. As explained above, these include forgiveness of sins, sanctification, eternal life, and everlasting blessedness, all of which Paul gathers together when he says in First Corinthians 1.
 Christ has been made for us by God our wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption. These words likewise speak of the whole Person of Christ, embracing
 both His merit and the forgiveness of sins, for redemption is expressly named. Such sayings are precious when we recognize the forgiveness of sins within them, because without this consolation we cannot stand before God. We must first consider and grasp in faith the article of the Creed, "I believe in the forgiveness of sins." When we thus comfort ourselves, it is also true that God genuinely dwells within us and the Lord Christ Himself works within us, as He says, "I give them eternal life," and, "No one will snatch them from My hands."
 From all this I conclude that I do not exclude Christ's merit or the forgiveness of sins when Paul says that we are justified through faith in Christ. I therefore retain this form in my prayer: Almighty and true God, eternal and only Father of Jesus Christ, Creator of heaven and earth, of angels, human beings, and all creatures, together with Your only-begotten Son Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit: have mercy on me, forgive my sins, and make me righteous for the sake of Your Son Jesus Christ and through Him, and sanctify me by Your Holy Spirit. Augustine also says that the whole certainty of our confidence rests in Christ's precious blood.
 I also wonder why Osiander rejects the explanation commonly given in our churches: that the faith of which Paul speaks when he says, "Being justified by faith, we have peace," is confidence in the mercy promised for the sake of the Mediator. Surely we must distinguish between the faith even the devils possess and the faith that receives the promise, whereby the heart gains consolation and joy. Such faith is knowledge of divine mercy and trust in Christ, of which Psalm 2 says, "Blessed are all who put their trust in Him." Wherever the prophets speak of God's mercy, they speak of this faith.
 Let this suffice, in this brief treatment, to remind many troubled people that they are to seek consolation in the Lord Christ, God and man, through faith; and that in this consolation God truly dwells and works within us. Anyone who observes this order can better understand the actual dispute and will not be led astray by strange theological arguments.
 I have passed over many other matters in order to avoid greater contention. May the Lord Jesus Christ graciously govern us. Amen.
 Because Dr. Osiander cites sayings of Luther, readers should understand that Luther sometimes speaks of the effective working of righteousness and elsewhere of consolation. They should therefore examine his expositions of the Psalms "Have mercy on me" and "Out of the depths," his discussion of the Psalm beginning "Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven," his sermons on John chapter 16, and his propositions against the Antinomians. Sixteen years ago I myself put questions to him and asked him to write out his answers plainly in his own hand. Although at that time our controversy was with the papists, one can nevertheless discern from those answers what Luther believed.
 Philipp Melanchthon, signed in his own hand.
