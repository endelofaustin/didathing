Martin Luther. Preface to the Commentary on Galatians. Written in 1535.

I can scarcely believe that I was as long-winded when I lectured publicly on this Epistle of Saint Paul to the Galatians as this little book now makes me appear. Yet I recognize as my own all the thoughts which the brethren have recorded here with such diligence. I must therefore admit that I said all these things—and perhaps even more—in those public lectures.

For one article reigns in my heart: faith in Christ. From this, through this, and toward this all my theological thoughts flow and return, day and night. And yet I find that I have grasped only the poorest beginnings and a few fragments of wisdom so high, so wide, and so deep.

I am therefore ashamed that my poor and cold reflections upon such a great Apostle and chosen instrument of God should be published. Yet I am driven to put shame aside and to be bold by the boundless and dreadful profanation which has always raged, and still rages, in the church of God against this one firm rock: the article of justification. That is, we are redeemed from sin, death, and the devil and given eternal life—not by ourselves, nor certainly by our works, which are less than ourselves, but by the help of another: the only-begotten Son of God, Jesus Christ.

Satan attacked this rock in Paradise. He persuaded our first parents that by their own wisdom and power they might become like God, and so they abandoned faith in the God who had given them life and promised to preserve it. Soon afterward that liar and murderer, always true to his nature, drove one brother to kill the other. His only reason was that the godly brother, by faith, had offered a better sacrifice, while the ungodly brother, offering his own works without faith, had not pleased God.

There followed a relentless and unbearable persecution of that same faith by Satan through the sons of Cain, until God was compelled to cleanse the world once through the Flood and to protect Noah, the preacher of faith and righteousness. Nevertheless, Satan kept his seed in Ham, Noah's third son. But who could recount all these things? Afterward the whole world went mad against this faith, inventing idols and religions without number. In these, each person went his own way, as Paul says, hoping by his own works to appease a god, a goddess, gods, or goddesses. In other words, without the help of Christ, who comes from outside ourselves, people hoped by their own works to redeem themselves from evils and sins. The deeds and writings of every nation testify sufficiently to this.

Yet these things are as nothing compared with what happened among that people of God, Israel, or the synagogue. Above all other peoples they were given the sure promise made to the fathers and the law handed down by God through angels. They also had the continual witness of the words, miracles, and examples of the prophets. Even among them, however, Satan—that is, the fury of self-righteousness—prevailed so greatly that, after killing the prophets, they killed even the Son of God, the Messiah promised to them. The reason was the same: the prophets and the Messiah taught that people please God by God's grace, not by their own righteousness.

From the beginning, this has been the central claim of the devil and the world: 'We do not want to appear to do wrong. Whatever we do, God must approve, and all his prophets must agree. If they will not agree, let them die. Let Abel perish and Cain live. This shall be our law.' And so it happens.

In the church of the Gentiles matters have been, and still are, so serious that the fury of the synagogue might seem mere child's play by comparison. For they did not recognize their Christ, as Paul says, and so they crucified the Lord of glory. But the Gentile church has received Christ and confesses that he is the Son of God, who has become our righteousness. It openly sings this, reads it, and teaches it. Yet while maintaining this confession, those very people who wish to be called the Church kill, persecute, and rage against people who believe, teach, and practice nothing other than what their persecutors themselves are compelled to confess with their mouths, though falsely: that Christ truly is our righteousness.

For today they rule under the name of Christ. If they could keep their power without his name, they would show outwardly what they think of him in their hearts. They think less of him even than those who regarded him as a thief deservedly crucified. Our own people treat him as a fable, like one of the imaginary gods of the nations. This can be seen in Rome, in the papal court, and almost everywhere in Italy.

Since Christ has therefore become a mockery among those who wish to be called his Christians; since Cain continues to kill Abel without end; and since the abomination of Satan holds such power, we must handle this article with the greatest possible diligence and set it against Satan. We must do so whether we are eloquent or inarticulate, learned or unlearned. Indeed, if every human voice fell silent, this rock would have to be proclaimed by the very rocks and stones themselves.

So I gladly do my duty and allow this exceedingly lengthy commentary to be published, in order to awaken my brethren in Christ against Satan's schemes and malice. In these last and final times he has been driven into such a rage by the restoration of the sound knowledge of Christ that, whereas before men seemed possessed by demons and raving, now the demons themselves seem possessed by still worse demons and to rage beyond all demonic fury. This is a strong sign that the enemy of truth and life senses that the day is near: a dreadful day of destruction for him, but a lovely day of redemption for us, when his tyranny will end.

He is not troubled without cause when all his members and powers are shaken. He is like a thief or adulterer discovered at dawn, when the rising light exposes him in the very act.

Who has ever heard—leaving aside the abominations of the pope—of so many monstrous outbreaks all at once as we see today even among the Anabaptists alone? In them Satan seems to be stirring up his followers everywhere with dreadful upheavals, as though he were breathing out the last breath of his kingdom. Through them he seems determined not only to overturn the world with revolts, but also, through innumerable sects, to swallow up Christ and his church altogether.

He does not rage in this manner against other ways of life or opinions—for example, against adulterers, thieves, murderers, perjurers, the godless, those who commit sacrilege, or unbelievers. Rather, he leaves them at peace in his own court, flatters them, and indulges them in everything. In the earliest days of the church he not only allowed all the idolatries and religions of the world to remain undisturbed; he positively fostered them, while harassing the one church and religion of Christ on every side. Later he let all heretics enjoy peace while troubling only the catholic—that is, universal—doctrine. So today he has no other occupation than the one that has always been especially his own: to persecute our Christ, who is our righteousness apart from all our works. As Scripture says, 'You shall strike at his heel.'

These reflections of mine on Saint Paul's Epistle are being published not so much against such opponents as for our own people. In the Lord they will either thank me for my diligence or forgive my weakness and boldness. I have no desire for the ungodly to approve these thoughts. Rather, I would have them and their god be provoked by them. For with much labor I have offered them only to those to whom Paul himself wrote this Epistle: those who are distressed, afflicted, harassed, and tempted—poor Galatians in faith. They alone understand these things.

Those who are not such people may listen instead to the papal party, the monks, the Anabaptists, and the many other teachers of boundless wisdom and a religion of their own making. Let them despise our words vigorously and make no attempt to understand them.

For today the papal party and the Anabaptists agree, even if they disguise it in words, on one proposition against the church of God: that God's work depends upon the worthiness of the person. Thus the Anabaptists teach, 'Baptism is nothing unless the person believes.' From this principle it necessarily follows, as they say, that all God's works are nothing unless a person is good. Baptism is God's work; yet, according to them, a wicked person makes it cease to be God's work.

From this it would further follow that marriage, civil government, and service are works of God, but because the people involved are wicked, they are no longer God's works. Wicked people have the sun, moon, earth, water, air, and all the things placed under human care. But because these people are wicked and not godly, the sun would no longer be the sun, nor the moon, earth, water, and air what they truly are. The Anabaptists themselves have bodies and souls, and they have their rebaptism. But since they were not godly, by this argument they would have no real bodies or souls.

Similarly, their parents were not, by their own admission, truly married, because they had not been rebaptized. Thus all the Anabaptists would be illegitimate, and all their parents adulterers and fornicators. Nevertheless they inherit their parents' possessions, even while admitting, by their own reasoning, that they are illegitimate and without a claim to the inheritance.

Who cannot see here that the Anabaptists are not merely people possessed by demons, but demons themselves possessed by worse demons? In the same way, the papal party has not ceased, even today, to insist upon works and personal worthiness against grace. In this they strongly support their Anabaptist brothers, at least in their teaching. These foxes are joined by the tails, though their heads point in different directions. Outwardly they pretend to be great enemies of one another. Inwardly, however, they really think, teach, and defend the same thing against the one Savior, Christ, who alone is our righteousness.

Therefore, let whoever can cling to this one article. As for the others, who have suffered shipwreck, let us leave them to be carried wherever the sea and winds will take them, until they return to the ship or swim to the shore. But of the Anabaptists, more on another occasion, if Christ the Lord wills. Amen.

Addition to the Preface, from the revised edition of 1538.

The substance and conclusion of my complaint is this: we should hope for no rest or end to these disputes while Christ and Belial are unable to agree. One generation passes and another comes. If one heresy falls, another soon rises, for the devil neither sleeps nor slumbers. Although I am nothing, I have now served in Christ's ministry for twenty years, and I can truly testify that more than twenty sects have attacked me. Some have utterly collapsed; others still twitch like the severed limbs of insects.

But day by day Satan, that god of divisive people, raises up new ones. Most recently he has raised up a group I least expected and could scarcely have imagined: those who contend that the Ten Commandments must be removed from the church, and that people should not be frightened by the law but gently admonished only by the grace of Christ. Thus they fulfill the prophecy of Micah, 'Do not preach to us.' It is as if we did not know, or had never taught, that afflicted and contrite spirits must be raised up through Christ, while stubborn Pharaohs, to whom the grace of God is preached in vain, must be terrified by the law. Even these people are compelled to invent revelations of wrath against the wicked and unbelieving, as though the law could be anything other than the revelation of wrath. So great are the blindness and arrogance of these people who oppose the law.

Therefore ministers of the Word must be certain, if they wish to be found faithful and wise on the day of Christ, that Saint Paul did not speak subtly or prophesy about some trivial matter when he said, 'There must be factions among you, in order that those who are genuine may be recognized.' Let Christ's minister know, I say, that as long as he teaches Christ purely, there will always be perverse people, even from among our own number, who strive to disturb the church.

Let him strengthen himself with this consolation: there can be no peace between Christ and Belial, nor between the offspring of the serpent and the offspring of the woman. Indeed, let him rejoice that he must endure these sects and rebellious spirits constantly succeeding one another. For this is our glory: the witness of our conscience that we are found standing and fighting on the side of the woman's offspring against the offspring of the serpent. Let the serpent strike at our heel, and let him never cease to strike.

For our part, we shall never cease to crush his head through Christ, the chief crusher of that serpent, who is blessed forever. Amen.
